## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do five things without notes. First, compute an arithmetic mean from a raw list, from a frequency table and from two groups of different sizes. Second, explain in your own words why the mean is the balance point of the data and what "balance" actually means here. Third, prove that the mean is the value that minimises the sum of squared deviations, and understand why that property makes it the backbone of regression and ANOVA. Fourth, decide in a concrete case whether the mean represents the data honestly or is misleading you, and name the right alternative. Fifth, detect and fix the most common mistake of all: averaging averages without checking how many observations sit behind each one.

The prerequisites are modest but worth checking. You need to add and divide fluently, decimals included. You need to read a fraction as a division and a percentage as a fraction of one hundred. You need to be comfortable with summation notation: $\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ simply means "add every value from the first to the last". There is no magic in the symbol, only a shortcut that saves you from writing "plus" twenty times. And you need to distinguish a quantitative variable from a categorical one, because a mean only makes sense for numbers that can be added.

A thirty-second entry check. If your grades are 6, 7 and 8, what is the mean? If you answered 7, you are ready. If you hesitated, the problem is not this topic: review addition and division first, because everything below rests on them. If you answered 21, you added and forgot to divide. That is exactly the kind of slip the mean punishes, and we will meet it again in the errors section.

One warning about scope. The arithmetic mean is the most famous measure of central tendency, but it is neither the only one nor always the right one. In this lesson we study it in depth, and by the end you will know precisely when to abandon it. Learning a tool includes learning its limits.

## 2. Why it matters

The mean shows up so often in daily life that it has become invisible. Your grade point average, the average temperature of a month, the average salary of a country, the average speed of a trip, the average spend per customer, the average height of a generation, the average waiting time in an emergency room. Behind every one of those phrases there is a decision: someone chose to compress hundreds or millions of numbers into one so that they could talk about them.

That compression has consequences. When a government announces that the average salary rose by 3%, many people conclude that their own salary rose by 3%. But the mean can rise because the highest salaries rose a lot while most people stagnated or fell. The number is correct; the interpretation is false. The gap between those two things is exactly what this lesson trains you to see.

It also matters because the mean is the doorway to almost everything else. Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean. Standard deviation is its square root. Regression estimates a line that always passes through the point formed by the means of both variables. Analysis of variance compares group means against a grand mean. The standard error of an estimator depends on the sample mean. If you do not understand what the mean does and does not do, everything that follows becomes a recipe you apply without knowing why.

There is a concrete practical cost to getting it wrong. Imagine you are in charge of purchasing and you compute a supplier's average price by averaging the prices of three orders — one of 10 units, one of 100 and one of 1,000 — without weighting by quantity. The number you get is not the price you pay per unit; it is an average of three prices that treats a tiny order and a huge order as equals. You negotiate with that number and lose money. The mean does not fail because it is difficult. It fails when it is applied without checking what each number represents.

And there is a deeper, almost philosophical reason. The mean answers a very specific question: which single value, repeated in place of every observation, would preserve the same total? That question is the right one when the total is what matters, and the wrong one when the typical case is what matters. Learning to ask that question before computing is the real goal of this lesson.

## 3. Intuition

Picture a massless wooden plank with five weights placed along a ruler. If you support the plank at a single point, where must your finger go so that it does not tip? At the point where the weight on one side balances the weight on the other. That point is the mean. This is not a decorative metaphor: it is the same equation written in the language of physics.

The key is that each observation contributes a "moment" equal to its signed distance from the support. A value below the support pulls left; a value above pulls right. Balance is reached when the sum of signed distances is zero. There is exactly one such point, because if you move the support slightly to the right, every distance on the left grows and every distance on the right shrinks, so the imbalance increases in one direction and never returns to zero.

Let us do it with numbers. Take 2, 4 and 9. Their mean is $(2+4+9)/3 = 5$. The signed distances from 5 are $-3$, $-1$ and $+4$. They add to zero. The two small values together pull left with force 4; the large value pulls right with force 4. Balance. If you chose 4 instead, the distances would be $-2$, $0$ and $+5$: still pulling right, the support is too far left. If you chose 6, they would be $-4$, $-2$ and $+3$: now pulling left. The only neutral point is 5.

This image explains three properties at once. First: if you add a very large value, the balance point shifts a long way toward it, because its moment is enormous. That is sensitivity to outliers. Second: if every value is doubled, every distance doubles, so the balance point doubles. The mean is linear. Third: if you add a value exactly equal to the mean, the point does not move, because its distance is zero. The mean is the only value that leaves the mean unchanged when you append it to the list.

Now the nuance that separates understanding from calculating. The plank balances *signed* distances, that is, distances with a sign. A single huge value on one side can offset many moderate values on the other. The mean does not look for the point "closest to everyone"; it looks for the point where the forces cancel. If you want the point that minimises distance to all observations without letting signs cancel, that point is not the mean: it is the median. The mean minimises the sum of *squares*, and squaring removes the sign by making everything positive rather than by cancelling.

An extreme case fixes the idea. Data: 1, 1, 1, 1, 96. The mean is 20. No observation is close to 20. The balance point sits at 20 because the 96 pulls right with force 76 while each of the four ones pulls left with force 19. The mean describes the total perfectly — the sum is 100, and five values of 20 also sum to 100 — but it describes the typical individual terribly. That duality, yes to the total and no to the individual, is the central intuition of the whole lesson.

There is a third image that helps when data arrive in a table. Think of the mean as an equal split: pool everything and divide it into equal shares; each share is the mean. Five friends earned 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. If they pool the money and split it equally, each receives 58.4 thousand. The split is fair in the sense that everyone gets the same, but it is absurd as a description of what each one earned, because four of them earned less than half of their share. The mean can always be read as "what each one would get if the total were shared equally". That reading clarifies when it is appropriate: when the total is what matters and the equal split is a useful fiction.

## 4. Definition and notation

For a sample of $n$ observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the arithmetic mean is defined as

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$$

The symbol $\bar{x}$ is read "x bar" and denotes a sample mean. When we refer to the whole population, the usual symbol is $\mu$ (mu), with the same definition over $N$ elements: $\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i$. The distinction between $n$ and $N$ is not pedantry: in practice you almost never have the population, you have a sample, and that difference is what justifies the whole of inferential statistics. In this lesson we compute the same way in both cases, but the symbols should not be confused.

The elements of the definition, one at a time. $x_i$ is each individual observation, with its unit: euros, centimetres, seconds, points. $n$ is the number of observations, a positive integer. $\sum$ is the summation sign, running over every index from 1 to $n$. Dividing by $n$ is what turns a sum into an average. The mean carries the same units as the data: measure in centimetres and the mean is in centimetres; measure in euros and the mean is in euros. This sounds obvious, but it is the first sanity check: a mean whose units differ from the data is a calculation error.

The notation for grouped or frequency data is a variant of the same formula. If the value $x_j$ appears $f_j$ times, with $k$ distinct values, then

$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} f_j\,x_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} f_j}$$

The denominator is the sum of frequencies, that is, $n$. This is not a new formula: it is the same one, grouping repeated terms. Multiplying 8 by 5 is just adding 8 five times. Use this version whenever the data arrive as a table, because it requires fewer additions and produces fewer mistakes.

An important special case is the weighted mean. If each observation carries a weight $w_i$ that is not a frequency but an importance or a size, the general formula is

$$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}$$

When all weights are equal, it collapses to the arithmetic mean. When the weights are frequencies, it collapses to the previous formula. When the weights are course credits, or the number of employees of a branch, or the population of a province, the formula is still the correct one and the simple mean is wrong. This is the generalisation most used in practice and the one most often forgotten.

Two limiting cases worth stating. If $n=1$, the mean is the only value; there is nothing to average and the formula gives $x_1/1 = x_1$. If every value equals a constant $c$, the mean is $c$: the sum is $nc$ and dividing by $n$ leaves $c$. If $n=0$ the mean is undefined; dividing by zero is not an average of nothing, it is an error, and serious software returns a missing value rather than zero.

A note on negative values. The mean works the same way with negative numbers, because addition and division are defined for them. What changes is the reading of the balance point: negative values pull left of zero and positive values pull right. With temperatures of $-5$, $0$ and $+8$ degrees, the mean is $+1$ degree, and balance is reached because $-5$ and $0$ pull down with combined force 6 while $+8$ pulls up with force 7 around the point $+1$. Check the distances: $-6$, $-1$ and $+7$, which sum to zero. The formula needs no adjustment; the intuition does.

One more note on censored or truncated data. If you measure income and the questionnaire offers a "more than 100,000" category, you do not have the exact value, and a mean computed with the lower bound underestimates the true mean. That is not a flaw in the formula; it is a flaw in the data. A mean can only average what it knows.

## 5. Step-by-step derivation

The mean was not invented for no reason. It comes from solving a very concrete optimisation problem: find the value $c$ that best represents the set, measuring error as the sum of squared distances. Let us derive it, because the derivation explains why it appears everywhere.

**Step 1. Define the error.** For a candidate $c$, the error of representing $x_i$ by $c$ is $x_i - c$. If we simply added those errors, positives and negatives would cancel, and one huge overshoot would offset one huge undershoot, which is absurd: two large errors are not better than none. To prevent that, we square them. The function to minimise is

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2$$

**Step 2. Expand.** Opening the square,

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i^2 - 2c\,x_i + c^2\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2 - 2c\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i + n c^2$$

**Step 3. Differentiate and set to zero.** The derivative with respect to $c$ is

$$S'(c) = -2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i + 2nc$$

Setting it to zero gives $2nc = 2\sum x_i$, hence $c = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i = \bar{x}$.

**Step 4. Confirm it is a minimum.** The second derivative is $S''(c) = 2n$, positive for any $n$ greater than zero. The function is an upward parabola, so the critical point is the global minimum, not a maximum or a saddle. The mean is literally the value that minimises the sum of squared deviations. Nothing more and nothing less.

**Step 5. Read the result.** Three consequences follow, and we will use all three throughout the course. The first is the balance equation: at the minimum the derivative is zero, which means $-2\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$, that is, $\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$. The deviations from the mean sum to exactly zero. This identity lets you recover a missing value and guarantees that residuals from a regression with an intercept sum to zero. The second is the Pythagorean decomposition: for any $c$,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - c)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 + n(\bar{x} - c)^2$$

The total error from using $c$ is the error from using the mean plus a term that depends only on the distance between $c$ and the mean. Since the second term is never negative, the mean is optimal. The third is linearity: if you transform each observation as $y_i = a x_i + b$, then $\bar{y} = a\bar{x} + b$. Summing gives $\sum(a x_i + b) = a\sum x_i + nb$, and dividing by $n$ leaves $a\bar{x} + b$. This is what allows unit changes without recomputing everything.

**Step 6. See what is lost.** The derivation assumes error is measured by squares. That assumption has a price: an error of 10 weighs one hundred times more than an error of 1, so a single far-away observation dominates the function. Had we used absolute values, the function would be $\sum|x_i - c|$ and its minimiser would be the median, not the mean. Had we used fourth powers, the minimiser would move even closer to the extremes. There is no "true" error measure: there is a choice, and each choice defines a different measure of central tendency. The mean answers squared error; the median answers absolute error; the mode answers counting error. Understanding this once saves you from memorising three separate definitions.

**Step 7. Derive the combined mean.** A case used constantly and almost never derived. You have two groups, the first with $n_1$ observations and mean $\bar{x}_1$, the second with $n_2$ observations and mean $\bar{x}_2$. What is the mean of the whole? The total sum is $n_1\bar{x}_1 + n_2\bar{x}_2$ and the total count is $n_1 + n_2$. Therefore

$$\bar{x}_{12} = \frac{n_1\bar{x}_1 + n_2\bar{x}_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

It is not the average of the two means unless $n_1 = n_2$. The formula extends to $k$ groups by summing in both numerator and denominator. It is the same weighted-mean formula, with group sizes as weights.

**Step 8. Derive the effect of one new observation.** If you have a mean $\bar{x}$ computed from $n$ observations and you add a new value $x_{n+1}$, the new mean is

$$\bar{x}_{\text{new}} = \frac{n\bar{x} + x_{n+1}}{n+1} = \bar{x} + \frac{x_{n+1} - \bar{x}}{n+1}$$

This form is the most useful in practice, because it avoids re-adding everything. It also quantifies sensitivity: the shift is the distance between the new value and the mean, divided by $n+1$. With large $n$, one new value barely moves the mean; with small $n$, it moves a lot. A value 100 units away from the mean shifts it by 20 in a sample of 4, and by 0.25 in a sample of 400. That is why means from large samples are stable and means from small samples are fragile.

**Step 9. Summarise the properties.** The mean is linear, its deviations sum to zero, it minimises the sum of squares, it combines by weighting with group sizes and it updates by the step-8 formula. Its breakdown point is zero: a single observation going to infinity takes the mean with it. That fragility is not an implementation defect; it is the inevitable consequence of choosing squared error. Anyone who wants robustness must change the error function, and that means changing the measure.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Salaries and the effect of one extreme value

Five people earn, in thousands of euros per year, 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200. The sum is $20+22+24+26+200 = 292$. Dividing by 5 gives a mean of $58.4$ thousand euros. Check the balance: the deviations are $-38.4$, $-36.4$, $-34.4$, $-32.4$ and $+141.6$. The four negatives sum to $-(38.4+36.4+34.4+32.4) = -141.6$, which cancels the positive exactly. The arithmetic is correct and the summary is useless: four of the five people earn less than half the mean, and nobody earns anywhere near 58.4. The median, the middle value of the sorted list, is 24. The mean describes the total; the median describes the typical person. A headline reading "average salary: 58,400 euros" tells the truth about the total and a lie about the people.

### Example 2. Mean from a frequency table

Exam grades for 20 students arrive as a table.

| Grade | Students | Product |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 2 | 8 |
| 6 | 5 | 30 |
| 8 | 8 | 64 |
| 10 | 5 | 50 |

The sum of products is $8+30+64+50 = 152$ and the number of students is $2+5+8+5 = 20$. The mean is $152/20 = 7.6$. The classic mistake is to average the distinct grades: $(4+6+8+10)/4 = 7$. That 7 treats the grade 4, held by only two students, as equal to the grade 8, held by eight, and therefore lands too low. The correct mean weights by frequency, which is the same as counting each student once. Rule of thumb: when data are grouped, the denominator is the sum of frequencies, never the number of table rows.

### Example 3. Combining two groups of different sizes

Two shifts in a factory produce parts with a mean error of 6.5 mm in shift A, with 30 parts, and 8.1 mm in shift B, with 50 parts. The combined mean is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{30 \times 6.5 + 50 \times 8.1}{30+50} = \frac{195 + 405}{80} = \frac{600}{80} = 7.5 \text{ mm}$$

The simple average of the two means would give $(6.5+8.1)/2 = 7.3$, which is wrong because shift B has 20 more parts and must weigh more. The difference is small here, but it can be huge: with 2 parts in shift A and 200 in shift B, the simple average would still give roughly 7.3 while the true mean would be close to 8.1. In any report with unequal groups, this formula is mandatory.

### Example 4. Finding a missing value

The mean of four numbers is 12. Three of them are 8, 10 and 15. What is the fourth? If the mean of four is 12, the total sum is $4 \times 12 = 48$. The three known values sum to $8+10+15 = 33$. The fourth is $48 - 33 = 15$. Check: $(8+10+15+15)/4 = 48/4 = 12$. This procedure underlies every "target mean" exercise and the simplest form of missing-data imputation: if you know the mean and every value but one, the missing one is determined. It is also why the mean offers no privacy: publishing the mean and $n-1$ values reveals the last one.

### Example 5. The speed trap

A car drives 120 km at 60 km/h and returns along the same road at 120 km/h. What is its average speed? The temptation is to average 60 and 120 and answer 90 km/h. That is false. Average speed is total distance divided by total time. The outbound trip takes $120/60 = 2$ hours; the return takes $120/120 = 1$ hour. Total time is 3 hours and total distance is 240 km, so the average speed is $240/3 = 80$ km/h. The reason 90 fails is that the car spends twice as long at 60 as at 120, and the arithmetic mean ignores that time. The correct mean for speeds over equal distances is the harmonic mean: $2/(1/60 + 1/120) = 2/(3/120) = 80$. Moral: before averaging, ask what each value weighs. If time is what gets distributed, the arithmetic mean of speeds is not the average speed.

### Example 6. Updating a mean without recomputing

A shop has 40 customers with an average spend of 25 euros. A new customer spends 85 euros. The new mean is $25 + (85-25)/41 = 25 + 60/41 \approx 26.46$ euros. Direct check: the previous total was $40 \times 25 = 1000$; the new total is $1085$; divided by 41 gives $26.46$. This update rule is what systems use to maintain a running mean without storing every observation, and it is the same idea behind the exponential moving averages used in machine learning.

### Example 7. Trimmed mean as an alternative

Back to the salaries 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200. A 20% trimmed mean removes 20% of the values from each end. With five observations, 20% is one observation, so the 20 and the 200 are dropped and the remaining three are averaged: $(22+24+26)/3 = 24$. Here the result coincides with the median, but it need not in general: the trimmed mean uses more information and is more stable when several extremes are present. What matters is that the trimming rule is declared before looking at the data and applied identically to every group; trimming only what annoys you is cosmetics, not analysis.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Believing the mean is the typical value.** The mean is the value that equalises the total, not the value that resembles most observations. In the salary example the mean is 58.4 and 80% of people earn below it. In skewed distributions — salaries, house prices, waiting times, follower counts — the mean is systematically dragged toward the long tail. Detection: compare mean and median; a large gap signals skew and means the mean is not the "typical" value. Correction: use the median to describe the typical case and the mean to describe the total, and say which one you are reporting.

**Error 2. Averaging averages without weighting.** This is the most common mistake in reports and the easiest to detect. If you are given the mean grade of two classes and you average them without knowing how many students each has, the result is only correct when the groups are equal. Detection: always ask for each group's size before combining. Correction: use the combined-mean formula with sizes as weights. The same error appears when averaging unemployment rates across provinces, prices across branches or satisfaction across segments.

**Error 3. Averaging ratios and speeds arithmetically.** Ratios do not behave like quantities. If you average speeds, yields per hectare or prices per unit without looking at the denominators, you get a number that corresponds to nothing. Detection: ask what sits in the denominator and whether it is constant across observations. Correction: the harmonic mean when the numerator is held constant (equal distances, equal quantities); the geometric mean when factors multiply (returns, growth rates).

**Error 4. Deleting outliers without justification.** An extreme value may be a data-entry error, but it may also be the most informative case: the fraud, the critically ill patient, the customer who churns. Removing it because it "wrecks the mean" is selecting evidence after seeing it. Detection: inspect every extreme and decide what to do before computing. Correction: document the rule — symmetric trimming, winsorising, a robust model — and apply it identically in every group. If the rule cannot be justified, report the mean with and without the extreme.

**Error 5. Treating mean and median as synonyms when the distribution is symmetric.** In a perfectly symmetric distribution, mean, median and mode coincide, and that is why many people use the three words interchangeably. But symmetry is a strong property that real data rarely satisfy exactly, and a small skew is enough to separate them. Detection: look at a histogram or compare the two values. Correction: always name the measure you computed; "the average" on its own is ambiguous and, in a serious report, a defect.

**Error 6. Rounding before finishing.** If you round every value to the nearest unit before summing, the error accumulates and the mean can drift by several tenths. With 100 values rounded down, the mean can fall by almost half a unit. Detection: compare the result with the unrounded computation. Correction: keep every decimal in intermediate steps and round only the final result, stating the number of decimals.

**Error 7. Believing the sample mean is the population mean.** The sample mean is an estimate; it changes from sample to sample. Its standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, which is why means from small samples fluctuate. Saying "the mean is 7.5" with no interval is claiming more than the data allow. Detection: ask for the sample size and the variability. Correction: always report $n$ and a measure of dispersion alongside the mean; a mean without dispersion is a naked number.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The mean is the centre of gravity of nearly all of statistics. Variance is defined as the average squared deviation from the mean: $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$. Without the mean there is no variance, and without variance there is no standard deviation, no standard error and no confidence interval. In linear regression, the least-squares line always passes through the point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, and the total sum of squares decomposes around the mean: $\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2 = \sum(\hat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2 + \sum(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2$. In analysis of variance, the grand mean is the reference against which group means are compared. In hypothesis testing, the statistic compares the observed mean with the mean predicted under the null.

It also connects to probability. The expectation of a discrete random variable, $E[X] = \sum x_i\,p_i$, is a mean weighted by probabilities instead of frequencies. Replace probabilities with relative frequencies and the expectation becomes the sample mean. That continuity is why the properties of the mean carry over to probability: linearity of expectation, expectation of a constant, expectation of a sum. And it connects to inference: the central limit theorem says the sample mean is approximately normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, which is what allows intervals and tests to be built. The whole inferential machine rests on an operation that fits on one line.

In professional practice, the mean appears wherever the total matters. A hospital computes average length of stay to plan beds; a company computes average ticket to forecast revenue; a product team computes average usage to size servers. In those settings the mean is the right measure because the total is what gets managed. When the typical case or equity is what matters, the mean fails and must be replaced: median for incomes and waiting times, percentiles for latency, trimmed mean for competitions, geometric mean for returns.

The most useful transfer is the question you ask first. Before computing any average, ask three things: what does each number represent and in what unit, do all values weigh the same or do some have different sizes, and do I care about the total or the typical case? Those three answers determine whether the arithmetic mean is the right tool or whether you need a weighted, geometric, harmonic or median alternative. Computing is the last step, not the first.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Compute the mean of 7, 9, 13, 15 and 21. — **Solution:** the sum is 65 and there are 5 values, so the mean is 13. Balance check: the deviations are -6, -4, 0, +2 and +8, which sum to 0.
2. The mean of eight numbers is 15. One of them, equal to 40, is removed. What is the mean of the remaining seven? — **Solution:** the original sum is $8 \times 15 = 120$. Removing 40 leaves 80 spread over 7 values: $80/7 \approx 11.43$. The mean falls because we removed a value well above the mean.
3. A student scores 4 in a 3-credit course, 7 in a 5-credit course and 9 in a 2-credit course. What is the credit-weighted mean? — **Solution:** $(3\times4 + 5\times7 + 2\times9)/(3+5+2) = (12+35+18)/10 = 65/10 = 6.5$. The simple mean would give $20/3 \approx 6.67$, which overstates because the low grade carries more credits.
4. The values 10, 20, 30 and a fourth value have a mean of 25. What is the fourth value? — **Solution:** the total must be $4 \times 25 = 100$. The three known values sum to 60, so the fourth is 40. Check: $(10+20+30+40)/4 = 100/4 = 25$.
5. A courier drives from a warehouse to a customer at 40 km/h and returns along the same route at 60 km/h. What is the average speed for the round trip? — **Solution:** not 50. If the one-way distance is $d$, total time is $d/40 + d/60 = (3d+2d)/120 = 5d/120 = d/24$. The average speed is $2d/(d/24) = 48$ km/h, the harmonic mean of 40 and 60.
6. In two sentences, explain why the mean has a breakdown point of zero and what that implies for a report built on possibly erroneous data. — **Solution:** because a single value can shift it arbitrarily; one observation heading to infinity takes the mean with it. It implies you must inspect the extremes and report a robust measure alongside, such as the median or a trimmed mean.

**Map of five ideas.** First: the mean is the balance point of signed distances, which is why deviations sum to zero. Second: it minimises the sum of squares, which makes it the foundation of variance, regression and ANOVA. Third: it is linear, it combines by weighting with group sizes and it updates by an incremental formula. Fourth: its sensitivity to extremes is not a bug but the price of squared error; when that price is too high, you change the measure. Fifth: before averaging you must decide whether the total or the typical case matters, and that decision, not the arithmetic, determines whether the result is useful.

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without notes why $\sum(x_i-\bar{x})=0$, why the combined mean is not the average of the means, and why a single 200,000-euro salary ruins the interpretation of an average salary, then you own this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, return to the matching example: the formula is short, but the intuition is what stays.
