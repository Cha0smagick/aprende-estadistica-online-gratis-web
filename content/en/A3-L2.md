## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do six things without notes. First, sort a list of values from smallest to largest and compute its median, whether the count is odd or even. Second, explain in your own words what "the value that splits the data in two equal halves" means and why half the observations fall below and half above (or, for even counts, the two middle values bracket it). Third, compare the mean and median on the same dataset and decide which one better describes the "typical case" when outliers are present. Fourth, compute the median from a cumulative frequency table without listing every observation. Fifth, decide in a concrete situation whether the median is the right measure or whether the mean is preferable. Sixth, recognize that the median is robust to outliers and understand what that means in practice: a single enormous or tiny value does not displace it.

The prerequisites are basic but essential. You need to sort numbers, including decimals and negatives, from smallest to largest. You need to know what half of a quantity is and what happens when that half falls between two values. You need to understand the difference between a small sample (a few observations) and a large sample (many observations), because the procedure changes slightly when the total count is even. And you need to have read the previous lesson on the arithmetic mean, because much of the value of the median is understood by contrast with it.

A thirty-second entry check. Data: 3, 7, 1, 9, 5. What is the median? If you answered 5, you are ready: you sorted (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) and took the middle. If you answered 5 but did not sort first, be careful: the median requires sorting, and skipping that step gives random results. If you answered 4.9 (the mean), you have confused the two measures; this lesson exists precisely to separate them. If you answered anything else, review ordering numbers before continuing.

A note on scope. The median is the most robust measure of central tendency, but robustness does not mean it always applies. Learning the median includes learning when NOT to use it: when the total matters, the median does not preserve it; when you need algebraic follow-up calculations (variance, regression), the median becomes awkward; when you have categorical data, the median is not even defined. That boundary is what makes the lesson worthwhile.

## 2. Why it matters

The median shows up in places where the mean fails spectacularly, and often we do not notice until the damage is done. House prices, salaries, emergency room waiting times, rents, national wealth, used car prices: all are distributions with a long tail, where a few very high values pull the mean upward and leave most people behind. If a headline says "average house prices rose 8%", most people neither bought nor sold at that price; the mean was dragged up by luxury mansions and premium neighborhoods. The median, in contrast, tells you what the house of the typical person costs — the one exactly in the middle of the distribution.

It also matters because the median is the measure serious institutions use when they want to be honest. National statistics offices report the median income, not only the mean, because they know mean income lies in countries with inequality. Real estate portals show median prices by neighborhood. Insurers calculate premiums from claims distributions where the median is more stable than the mean. In data science, when you clean a dataset and find outliers, the median is the first tool you try for imputing missing values without being misled by extremes.

There is a concrete cost to using the median where the mean belongs, and vice versa. Imagine you manage a warehouse and need to know how much stock to buy to cover typical demand. If you use the mean and there was one exceptional month with a massive purchase, you overbuy and end up with excess inventory. If you use the median, you capture the typical month and avoid the surplus. Now imagine the opposite: you manage a company budget and need the expected total spending. Here the median is of little use; you need the mean, because it is the one that preserves the total. Using the wrong tool in each case is not a minor error: it is a business decision based on a number that does not represent what you think it represents.

And there is a subtler reason. The median forces you to sort the data, and sorting is the first step of almost every exploratory analysis. When you sort, you see the range, you see the extremes, you see gaps, you see clusters. The mean gives you a number and stops; the median forces you to look at the whole landscape before giving you a number. That willingness to look before summarizing is, in itself, a good analytical habit that carries over to anything you do with data afterward.

## 3. Intuition

Picture a line of people who agree to stand in order of height, from shortest to tallest. If there are five people, the median is the height of the person in the middle: the third. There are two shorter people to their left and two taller people to their right. It does not matter how tall the tallest person is or how short the shortest is; the median only cares that there are equal numbers on each side. If the tallest person grows twenty centimeters, the median does not move. If the shortest shrinks ten, neither does. That is robustness: the central value does not fear the extremes.

Now imagine six people. There is no exact middle person; there are two: the third and the fourth. The median is the average of their heights. This has an important consequence: the median can be a value that belongs to none of the original observations. If the values are 160, 165, 170, 175, 180 and 190 centimeters, the median is (170+175)/2 = 172.5, and nobody measures 172.5. That is not a defect; it is simply the point that divides the line into two equal halves of three people each.

An extreme case to fix the idea. Five people earn 1,000, 1,200, 1,500, 2,000 and 100,000 euros per month. The mean is 21,140 euros, and nobody earns anywhere near that. The median is 1,500 euros: the middle person. A millionaire joins the group and the mean shoots up to 17,000 euros; the median stays at 1,500, because there are still two people below and two above it. The median ignores the magnitude of the extreme and only counts how many are on each side. That is the central intuition: the median does not add, it only counts and sorts.

A third useful image. Think of the median as a balance point where it does not matter how much each extreme weighs, only how many objects are on each plate. If you put one hundred one-cent coins on one plate and a one-euro coin on the other, the mean moves toward the euro because it sums value; the median stays where the middle coin is, because it only counts coins. That explains why the median is the preferred measure when data have a "long tail": the tail pulls the mean, but the median does not feel it.

Finally, a nuance that separates understanding from calculating. The median is not "the most common value" (that is the mode) nor "the average value" (that is the mean). It is the value that occupies the central position once the data are sorted. If all values are equal, the median is that value. If all values are distinct, the median is the middle one. If there are repetitions, the median is still the middle one, even if that value repeats several times. The median does not care about frequencies; it cares about positions.

## 4. Definition and notation

Let a sample of $n$ observations be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$. To find the median, you first sort the values from smallest to largest and denote them $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$, where the parenthesis in the subscript indicates the order (order statistic). The median, denoted $\tilde{x}$ (read "x tilde"), is defined as:

$$\tilde{x} = \begin{cases} x_{\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\[6pt] \dfrac{x_{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)} + x_{\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}}{2} & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \end{cases}$$

When $n$ is odd, the median is simply the middle value: the observation in position $(n+1)/2$. When $n$ is even, there is no single middle value, so you take the mean of the two middle values, in positions $n/2$ and $n/2+1$.

The elements of the definition, one by one. $x_{(i)}$ is the i-th ordered observation, not the i-th observation as it arrived. That is crucial: if data arrive unsorted (as they usually do in real life), the first step is to sort them. The parenthesis notation reminds you that we are no longer talking about the original order, but about order of magnitude. $n$ is the total number of observations. There are no special units: the median has the same units as the original data.

For data grouped in a frequency table, the median is not computed with the simple formula above, but by locating the value or class where the cumulative frequency reaches 50%. If value $x_j$ has frequency $f_j$ and the cumulative frequency up to $x_j$ is $F_j = \sum_{k=1}^{j} f_k$, then the median is the smallest value $x_j$ such that $F_j \ge n/2$. In other words, you accumulate frequencies until you reach the midpoint of the total, and that is your median value. For continuous data grouped in intervals, linear interpolation within the median class is used, but that is an advanced case left for inference.

A limiting case: if $n=1$, the median is the only observation. If all values equal a constant $c$, the median is $c$, because the middle value (or the mean of the two middle values) is $c$. If $n=0$, the median is undefined, just like the mean: there is nothing to sort. Negative values present no problem: they are sorted the same way, from most negative to least negative, and the median falls where it should. For example, for -10, -5, 0, 3, 8, the median is 0.

Another precision: the median of a sample is not necessarily a member of the sample. As we saw with the even-count example, it can be an intermediate value that does not appear in the original data. That is perfectly valid and not an error. On the contrary, it signals that the median is doing its job: sitting exactly in the middle, even if that middle is not occupied by any real observation.

## 5. Step-by-step derivation and properties

The median is not derived by minimizing a differentiable error function like the mean; it derives from a counting property. Let us see why the median is the value that minimizes the sum of absolute deviations, and what that implies.

**Step 1. Define the absolute error.** For a candidate $c$, the error of representing each observation $x_i$ by $c$ is $|x_i - c|$, the absolute distance. Unlike the squared error of the mean, here we do not square the errors, so there is no extra penalty for large values. The function to minimize is

$$S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - c|$$

**Step 2. Analyze the behavior of the function.** If you move $c$ slightly to the right, all observations to the left of $c$ see their distance increase by the same amount, and all observations to the right see their distance decrease by that same amount. Observations exactly at $c$ do not change. Therefore, the change in $S(c)$ depends only on how many observations are on each side, not on how far away they are.

**Step 3. Find the minimum.** If there are more observations to the left of $c$ than to the right, moving $c$ to the right reduces $S(c)$ (you gain more on the right than you lose on the left). If there are more observations to the right, moving $c$ to the left reduces $S(c)$. The minimum is reached exactly when the number of observations on each side is equal, or as equal as possible. That happens precisely at the median.

**Step 4. Read the consequences.** Two key properties follow from this derivation. The first is robustness: since the function only counts how many observations are on each side and does not sum their distances, an extremely distant value has no more influence than a nearby one, as long as it stays on the same side. That explains why a millionaire does not move the median salary. The second is that the median is not unique in all cases: if $n$ is even, any value between the two middle values minimizes the sum of absolute deviations, and by convention we choose the mean of the two middle values.

**Step 5. Compare with the mean.** The mean minimizes the sum of squared errors, which heavily weights large errors. The median minimizes the sum of absolute errors, which treats all errors equally. That is the fundamental difference: the mean is the minimizer of squared error, the median is the minimizer of absolute error. Neither is "better" in the abstract; each answers a different definition of what "error" means.

**Step 6. Invariance under monotonic transformation.** If you apply an increasing monotonic transformation to all observations (for example, taking logarithms, or squaring for positive data), the median of the transformed data is the transformation of the original median. That does not hold for the mean: mean(log x_i) ≠ log(mean(x_i)). This property makes the median useful when working on transformed scales.

**Step 7. Breakdown point.** The median has a breakdown point of 50%: you need to corrupt more than half the data to move the median arbitrarily. The mean has a breakdown point of 0%: a single value is enough. That is why the median is the measure of trust when you suspect data entry errors, fraud, or outliers in your data.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Median with an odd number of observations

The waiting times (in minutes) of five patients in the emergency room are: 45, 12, 30, 60, 25. First sort them: 12, 25, 30, 45, 60. There are 5 observations (odd), so the median is the value in position $(5+1)/2 = 3$, which is 30 minutes. Check: two patients waited less than 30 minutes (12 and 25) and two waited more (45 and 60). The median describes the waiting time of the typical patient. The mean, by contrast, is $(12+25+30+45+60)/5 = 34.4$, which is higher because the two long waits (45 and 60) pull it up.

### Example 2. Median with an even number of observations

Six students' exam scores are: 6, 8, 4, 9, 7, 5. Sorted: 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. There are 6 observations (even), so the median is the mean of the two middle values, in positions 3 and 4: $(6+7)/2 = 6.5$. No student scored 6.5, but that is the value that divides the class into two halves of three students each. Three scored 6 or below and three scored 7 or above.

### Example 3. Median with repeated values

The prices of six used phones are: 150, 200, 200, 200, 300, 500 euros. They are already sorted. There are 6 observations, so the median is the mean of the two middle values (200 and 200): $(200+200)/2 = 200$ euros. Even though 200 repeats three times, the median is still 200 because it occupies the central position. This shows that the median does not depend on how many times a value repeats, but on its ordered position.

### Example 4. Median from a frequency table

A survey of 30 households about cars per month gives this table:

| Books per month | Students | Cumulative frequency |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 5 | 5 |
| 1 | 8 | 13 |
| 2 | 10 | 23 |
| 3 | 4 | 27 |
| 4 | 3 | 30 |

The total is $n=30$, so we look for the value where cumulative frequency reaches 50%, i.e., position 15. Cumulative frequency reaches 13 with 1 book and 23 with 2 books. Therefore position 15 falls in the group of 2 books, and the median is 2 books per month. Note: we did not need to list all 30 values one by one; the cumulative frequency table gives the answer directly.

### Example 5. Robustness against an outlier

Five companies have annual profits (in millions of euros): 2, 3, 4, 5 and 100. The mean is $(2+3+4+5+100)/5 = 22.8$ million, a number no company approaches. The median is 4 million, the middle value. If the 100 million company is a data entry error or an exceptional case, the mean tells you the "typical" profit is 22.8 million, which is false for four of the five companies. The median tells you 4 million, which does represent the typical set. Now change 100 to 1,000: the mean becomes 202.8 million, absurd; the median stays at 4 million. That is robustness in action.

### Example 6. Median with negative values

Daily stock variations (in %) are: -5, -2, 0, 3, 7. Sorted: -5, -2, 0, 3, 7. The median is 0, the middle value. Even with negative values, the procedure is identical: sort and take the middle. The median does not require positive data; it only requires that the data can be ordered.

### Example 7. When the median does not serve

You have the prices of five stocks: 10, 12, 11, 13 and 12 euros. The median is 12. Now you need the total value of the portfolio for the balance sheet. The median does not serve: $5 \times 12 = 60$ is not the real value, which is $10+12+11+13+12 = 58$. For the total you need the mean (or the sum directly). The median describes the typical case; the mean describes the total. That distinction is what decides which measure to use in each report.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Forgetting to sort before calculating.** The median requires sorted data. If you take the middle value of an unsorted list, the result is random. For example, for 30, 10, 20, the middle value in the original order is 10, but the true median is 20. Detection: always sort first; if you use a spreadsheet, apply the MEDIAN function after verifying the data, or better, let the function do the internal sorting and do not trust the visual order. Correction: sorting is step 1, not an optional extra.

**Error 2. Believing the median is always a sample member.** As we saw in the even-count case, the median can be an intermediate value that does not appear in the data (6.5 in the scores example). Some students think that if the result is not in the list, something is wrong. It is not. Detection: when $n$ is even, expect a value that may not be in the sample. Correction: report the median as is, with decimals if necessary, and do not force it to match an existing observation.

**Error 3. Using the median when you need the total.** The median does not preserve the sum. If you report the median spending of five departments and multiply by five, you do not get total spending. Detection: ask yourself whether you need the typical case or the aggregate total. Correction: use the mean for totals and budgets; use the median to describe the typical individual.

**Error 4. Confusing median with mode.** The mode is the most frequent value; the median is the middle ordered value. In a list like 1, 2, 2, 3, 100, the mode is 2 and the median is 2 (they coincide by chance), but in 1, 2, 3, 4, 100 the median is 3 and there is no mode. Detection: always name what you are computing. Correction: if the report says "the most frequent value is...", it is mode; if it says "the middle value is...", it is median.

**Error 5. Thinking the median is immune to everything.** The median is robust to outliers, but it is not magic. If more than half your data are wrong, the median will be wrong too. If all your data are equally extreme, the median is extreme. Detection: check data quality, not just the measure. Correction: use the median together with other measures (interquartile range, mode) for a complete picture.

**Error 6. Applying the median to unordered categorical data.** It makes no sense to compute the median of favorite colors (red, blue, green) because there is no natural order. The median requires ordinal or quantitative data. Detection: ask whether you can say one value is "greater" than another. Correction: for unordered categorical data, use the mode.

**Error 7. Rounding the median of an even sample.** If the two middle values are 7 and 8, the median is 7.5. Rounding to 7 or 8 biases the result toward one of the extremes. Detection: keep decimals in the median of even samples. Correction: report the exact median and round only when presenting, stating how many decimals you used.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The median is the basis of the interquartile range (IQR), defined as $Q_3 - Q_1$, where $Q_1$ is the median of the lower half and $Q_3$ is the median of the upper half. Without understanding the median, you do not understand the IQR, and without the IQR you do not understand boxplots or outlier detection using the 1.5 × IQR criterion. The median is, therefore, the starting point of all modern exploratory data analysis.

In inference, the median appears in non-parametric tests such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Mann-Whitney test, which compare medians (or more precisely, distributions) without assuming normality. When data are not normal — which is common in real life — these tests are more appropriate than t-tests based on means. The median is also the central estimator in quantile regression, where instead of modeling the conditional mean you model the conditional median or any other quantile.

In probability, the median of a random variable is the value $m$ such that $P(X \le m) \ge 0.5$ and $P(X \ge m) \ge 0.5$. It is the 0.5 quantile. For symmetric distributions, median and mean coincide; for skewed distributions, they diverge, and that divergence is a signal of skewness. Pearson's skewness coefficient uses precisely the difference between mean and median: $g_1 = 3(\bar{x} - \tilde{x})/s$.

In professional practice, the median is the standard in fields where extremes are common and costly: economics (incomes, wealth), real estate (house prices), insurance (claims), health (waiting times, hospital stays), and data science (dataset cleaning, missing value imputation). When you read a report that cites only the mean in one of these fields, be careful: it may be omitting the median precisely because that would reveal a more modest reality.

The most useful transfer is the habit of reporting both. In any serious analysis, present mean and median together. If they differ a lot, you have skewness and outliers; if they are similar, the distribution is reasonably symmetric. That comparison of two numbers tells you more about your data than either one alone.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Compute the median of 7, 3, 9, 1, 5. — **Solution:** sorted they are 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. $n=5$ (odd), the median is the value in position 3, which is 5.
2. Compute the median of 10, 20, 30, 40. — **Solution:** they are already sorted. $n=4$ (even), the median is $(20+30)/2 = 25$. No observation equals 25, but that is the midpoint.
3. The prices of seven houses are: 150,000, 180,000, 200,000, 220,000, 250,000, 800,000 and 1,200,000 euros. What are the median and the mean? Which better describes the typical house? — **Solution:** they are already sorted. $n=7$, the median is the value in position 4: 220,000 euros. The mean is $(150+180+200+220+250+800+1,200)/7 = 3,000/7 \approx 428,571$ euros. The median better describes the typical house; the mean is pulled up by the two expensive houses.
4. A frequency table of 30 students about books read per month: 0 books (5 students), 1 book (8 students), 2 books (10 students), 3 books (4 students), 4 books (3 students). What is the median? — **Solution:** $n=30$, we look for position 15. Cumulative frequencies: 5 (0 books), 13 (1 book), 23 (2 books). Position 15 falls in the group of 2 books. The median is 2 books per month.
5. The profits of five companies are: 2, 3, 4, 5 and 100 million. If the 100 is an error and should be 6, what happens to the median and the mean? — **Solution:** With 100, the median is 4 and the mean is 22.8. With 6, the data are 2, 3, 4, 5, 6; the median remains 4 and the mean drops to 4. The median did not move; the mean changed dramatically. That demonstrates the robustness of the median against changes in an extreme value.
6. Explain in two sentences why the median of an even sample can be a value that does not appear in the original data. — **Solution:** Because when there is an even number of observations, there is no single middle value; the median is defined as the mean of the two middle values, which can be an intermediate number not present in the sample. That is correct and not an error.
**Map of five ideas.** First: the median is the middle value of sorted data, and divides the sample into two equal halves. Second: it is computed by sorting and taking the middle (odd) or the mean of the two middle values (even). Third: it is robust to outliers because it only counts how many observations are on each side, not their magnitudes. Fourth: it minimizes the sum of absolute deviations, unlike the mean which minimizes the sum of squares. Fifth: use it to describe the typical case in skewed distributions; use it together with the mean to detect skewness.

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without notes why the median of 1, 2, 3, 4 is 2.5 although no observation equals 2.5, why a millionaire does not move the median salary, and when you would prefer the median over the mean in a report, then you own this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, return to the matching example: the formula is short, but the intuition of "middle value that does not fear extremes" is what stays.
