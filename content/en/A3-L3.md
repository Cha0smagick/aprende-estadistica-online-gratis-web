## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do seven things without notes. First, identify the mode of a dataset: the value or values that appear with the greatest frequency. Second, recognize that a dataset can have one mode (unimodal), two modes (bimodal), several modes (multimodal) or no mode at all, and know how to distinguish each case. Third, compute the mode for categorical data (colors, brands, survey answers) and for discrete quantitative data (whole numbers, counts). Fourth, identify the modal class in a grouped frequency table or histogram: the bin or interval with the most observations. Fifth, explain why the mode is the only measure of central tendency that makes sense for categorical data, and why the mean and median cannot be computed on them. Sixth, compare the mode with the mean and median on the same dataset and say what each one counts. Seventh, detect the frequent mistake of claiming that "the mode is useless for numbers" and correct it with concrete examples.

The prerequisites are light but worth checking. You need to count frequencies: knowing that the value 3 appears four times in a list. You need to understand what a category is (a group without numerical order: red, blue, green) and what a quantitative value is (a number with a unit: age, price, height). You need to read a simple frequency table and a grouped frequency table. And you should have read the lessons on the mean and the median, because the mode is best understood by contrast with them: while the mean averages and the median sorts, the mode counts.

A thirty-second entry check. Data: red, blue, red, green, blue, red. What is the mode? If you answered "red", you are ready: it appears three times, more than anything else. If you answered with a number, you have confused the data type; there are no numbers to average here, only categories to count. If you answered "there is no mode because they are all different", look again: red appears three times, so there is a mode. If you answered anything else, review frequency counting before continuing.

A note on scope. The mode is the easiest measure of central tendency to calculate and the most limited algebraically: it cannot be added, it cannot be used in variance or regression formulas, and in small samples it can be unstable (a single changed observation can displace it). Learning the mode includes learning when not to trust it as the only measure: it is best presented alongside the mean or median when the data are numerical.

## 2. Why it matters

The mode is the measure of central tendency most used in daily life, though it is rarely called by its name. When you say "the best-selling color", "the most common size", "the most frequent survey answer" or "the star product", you are talking about the mode. It answers the question: which value occurs most often? That is the question asked most often in business, marketing, public policy and data science, because it points directly to practical decisions: what to manufacture, what to stock, what to promote, what to prioritize.

It also matters because it is the only measure of central tendency that works with categorical data. If you have a list of favorite colors (red, blue, green, red, blue), it makes no sense to compute a mean or a median: you cannot add colors and there is no natural order. The mode, however, tells you that red and blue tie as the most frequent. Without the mode, categorical data would have no measure of "typical value" at all. That makes the mode indispensable in surveys, market research, social sciences and any field that works with multiple-choice answers.

There is a concrete cost to ignoring the mode. Imagine you manage clothing store inventory and only look at the mean size sold. If the mean comes out to M, but in reality you sell many S and many L and few M, you will overstock M and run out of the extremes. The mode would tell you exactly which sizes to restock first. The same applies to ice cream flavors, phone colors, subscription plans, or incident types in technical support: the mode points to where demand is concentrated, and concentrated demand is where money is made or lost.

And there is a deeper reason. The mode is the gateway to understanding distribution shape and data shape. When you look for the mode, you are looking for peaks: concentrations of observations at certain values. A dataset with two modes (bimodal) is telling you that there are two distinct groups in your data: for example, customers who buy little and customers who buy a lot, with few in the middle. Detecting that with the mean would be impossible (the mean would fall in the middle, where almost nobody is); with the mode, it jumps out. The mode is, therefore, the first tool for exploring the shape of a distribution.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine you have a bag of colored candies and you pull them out one by one, noting the color of each. At the end, you count how many candies of each color came out. The color that came out most often is the mode. It is the simplest and most direct answer to "what is most common?". You do not need to add, you do not need to sort, you do not need to average: you just count and pick the maximum.

Now imagine a tie: two colors came out the same number of times, and that is the maximum. Then the dataset is bimodal: it has two modes. There is no single "typical color"; there are two. If three colors tie, it is trimodal; if more, multimodal. The mode does not force a single answer; it accepts that several values can be equally typical.

An extreme case to fix the idea. Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Each value appears exactly once. There is no mode: no value is more frequent than another. If you add a 3, now the 3 appears twice and the others once; the mode is 3. A single added observation created a mode where there was none before. That shows that the mode can be unstable in small samples: a minimal change in the data can create, eliminate or move the mode. That instability is not a defect; it is a signal that the mode depends entirely on observed frequencies, and those frequencies can fluctuate.

A third useful image. Think of the mode as the peak of a mountain in a landscape of data. If you plot each value on a horizontal axis and stack as many units as times it appears, you get a histogram. The mode is the highest peak of that histogram. If there are two equally high peaks, there are two modes. If the landscape is flat (all values appear equally often), there is no mountain and no mode. That image helps you see the mode not as an abstract calculation but as a geographic shape of the data.

Finally, an important nuance. The mode is not "the largest value" nor "the smallest value"; it is the most repeated value. In a list like 100, 100, 1, 2, 3, the mode is 100 even though it is the largest value, because it appears twice and the others once. In 1, 1, 2, 100, 100, the mode is bimodal: 1 and 100. The magnitude of the value does not matter; only its frequency matters.

## 4. Definition and notation

For a sample of $n$ observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the mode is the value (or values) that appears with the greatest frequency. If we denote the frequency of a value $v$ as $f(v) = \#\{i : x_i = v\}$, then the mode is any value $m$ such that

$$f(m) = \max_{v} f(v)$$

That is, $m$ is a value whose frequency equals the maximum frequency among all distinct values in the sample. There is no single universal symbol for the mode; sometimes it is written $\hat{x}$ or simply "mode", and when there are several, we speak of modes $m_1, m_2, \dots$.

The elements of the definition, one by one. The frequency of a value is the number of times it appears in the sample. The maximum frequency is the highest of all those frequencies. The mode is any value that reaches that maximum frequency. If a single value reaches it, the distribution is unimodal. If two values reach it, it is bimodal. If more than two, multimodal. If all values appear the same number of times (for example, once each), there is no mode.

For quantitative continuous data grouped into intervals or bins (as in a histogram), no single value repeats exactly, so we speak of a modal class: the interval with the highest frequency. The exact mode within that class can be estimated by interpolation, but that is an advanced case. In practice, reporting the modal class is already enough to describe the peak of the distribution.

Two limiting cases. If $n=1$, the mode is the only value (its frequency is 1, which is the maximum). If all values are distinct, there is no mode (all frequencies are 1, so there is no unique maximum greater than the others). If all values are equal, the mode is that value (its frequency is $n$, clearly the maximum). Categorical data work the same way: the mode is the category with the most counts.

An important precision: the mode does not require the data to be numerical. It works equally well with categories, colors, brands, survey answers or any label. That makes it the only measure of central tendency applicable to nominal data, where neither the mean nor the median makes sense.

## 5. Properties and step-by-step comparison

The mode is not derived by minimizing an error function like the mean or the median; it derives directly from frequency counting. Still, it has important properties worth understanding.

**Property 1: it is the only measure applicable to categorical data.** The mean requires adding, and you cannot add colors or brands. The median requires sorting, and there is no natural order among colors. The mode only requires counting, so it works with any data that can be classified into categories. That is its greatest advantage and the reason it is omnipresent in surveys and market research.

**Property 2: it may not exist or may not be unique.** Unlike the mean (which always exists for numerical data) and the median (which always exists for sortable data), the mode may not exist (all values distinct) or may not be unique (several values tie for the maximum frequency). That makes it less predictable algebraically, but more honest: if there is no clear typical value, the mode does not invent one.

**Property 3: it is not robust in small samples.** A single added or changed observation can create, eliminate or displace the mode. In the list 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 there is no mode; add a 3 and the mode 3 appears; change that 3 to a 4 and the mode shifts to 4. That instability means that in small samples the mode should be interpreted carefully and, ideally, accompanied by the mean or median.

**Property 4: it is insensitive to the magnitude of values.** The mode only counts appearances, it does not sum values. An extremely large value appearing once does not influence the mode if another value appears twice. That makes it robust to outliers in the sense that an isolated extreme does not displace it, but it does not make it robust in the sense of the median (which ignores the magnitude of extremes but remains central).

**Property 5: it has no useful algebraic properties.** You cannot add modes, you cannot average modes of subgroups to get the mode of the total group, and modes do not appear in variance, standard deviation or regression formulas. That limits it as a tool for later calculation, but not as a tool for description.

**Comparison with the mean and the median.** In a symmetric, unimodal distribution, mean, median and mode are usually close or coincide. In a right-skewed distribution (salaries, house prices), typically mode < median < mean: the tail of high values pulls the mean upward, the median stays in the middle, and the mode stays at the lowest and most frequent peak. That relationship is a useful visual signal: if the three measures diverge a lot, the distribution is skewed.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Mode of categorical data

A survey of 20 people about their favorite color gives: red, blue, red, green, blue, red, green, blue, red, blue, green, red, blue, green, blue, red, blue, green, red, blue. Counting: red appears 7 times, blue 8 times, green 5 times. The mode is blue, with 8 appearances. It is the most frequent color, the typical answer of the survey. There is no mean or median of colors; the mode is the only measure possible.

### Example 2. Mode of discrete quantitative data

The number of children in 10 families is: 2, 3, 2, 1, 4, 2, 3, 2, 1, 5. Counting: 1 appears 2 times, 2 appears 4 times, 3 appears 2 times, 4 appears 1 time, 5 appears 1 time. The mode is 2, with 4 appearances. It is the most common number of children in this sample. Note: the mean is $(2+3+2+1+4+2+3+2+1+5)/10 = 25/10 = 2.5$, and the median sorting (1,1,2,2,2,2,3,3,4,5) is $(2+2)/2 = 2$. Here the three coincide or are close, but that is not always the case.

### Example 3. Bimodal distribution

The ages of 12 customers of a store are: 18, 19, 20, 65, 66, 67, 68, 19, 20, 65, 66, 67. Counting: 18 (1), 19 (2), 20 (2), 65 (2), 66 (2), 67 (2), 68 (1). The maximum frequency is 2, reached by 19, 20, 65, 66 and 67. This is multimodal (five modes). What it tells us is that there are two clear groups: young customers (18-20) and older customers (65-68), with few in the middle. The mean would be about 43 years, an age that practically no customer has; the median would also fall in the middle, where almost nobody is. The modes, in contrast, reveal the two real peaks.

### Example 4. No mode

The maximum temperatures of seven days are: 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 degrees. Each value appears exactly once. There is no mode. That is correct: there is no typical value more frequent than the others; all are equally rare. Reporting "no mode" is a valid and honest conclusion.

### Example 5. Modal class in grouped data

The incomes of 100 people grouped into intervals give this table:

| Income range (€) | People |
|---:|---:|
| 0 – 1,000 | 10 |
| 1,000 – 2,000 | 25 |
| 2,000 – 3,000 | 40 |
| 3,000 – 4,000 | 15 |
| 4,000 – 5,000 | 10 |

The modal class is 2,000 – 3,000 €, because it has the highest frequency (40 people). We do not know each person's exact income, but we know the largest concentration peak is there. For decisions like setting a support threshold or a product price, that information is already valuable.

### Example 6. The mode is not fooled by an outlier

The prices of 10 houses are: 150,000, 160,000, 170,000, 180,000, 190,000, 200,000, 210,000, 220,000, 230,000 and 2,000,000 euros. The mode does not exist (all appear once) or, if we group by tens of thousands, the peak is in the 150,000–230,000 range. The mean is $(150+160+170+180+190+200+210+220+230+2,000)/10 = 3,710/10 = 371,000$ euros, a figure no house approaches. The median is $(200+210)/2 = 205,000$. The mode (if grouped) reflects the typical price group; the mean is destroyed by the 2 million mansion. This is the same pattern as with the median, but the mode does it without even sorting.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Saying that "the mode is useless for numbers".** It is false. The mode works perfectly for discrete numerical data (number of children, number of cars, goals in a match). What does not work for ungrouped continuous numbers is the exact mode, because it is unlikely that two values are identical to the thousandth; there you use the modal class. Detection: if someone dismisses the mode as "numerical", they are confusing ungrouped continuous data with discrete data. Correction: use the mode for discrete and categorical data; use the modal class for grouped continuous data.

**Error 2. Believing there is always a mode.** As we saw, there may not be one (all values distinct) or there may be several (bimodal, multimodal). Stating "the mode is X" without checking for a tie is an error. Detection: always count the frequencies of all values before declaring a mode. Correction: report all modes if there is a tie, or declare "no mode" if all frequencies are equal.

**Error 3. Confusing the mode with the largest or smallest value.** The mode is the most repeated, not the most extreme. In 100, 100, 1, 2, 3 the mode is 100 even though it is the maximum, because it appears twice. Detection: check that the value reported as mode is actually the one with the highest frequency, not the one with the greatest magnitude. Correction: name it explicitly as "most frequent value" rather than "typical value" without more.

**Error 4. Using the mode alone for numerical data.** The mode does not reliably describe the center of a numerical distribution; it only describes the most frequent peak. In a skewed distribution, the mode can be far from the center of gravity (the mean). Detection: if a report cites only the mode for numerical data without mean or median, it is incomplete. Correction: for numerical data, always report mode together with mean and median for comparison.

**Error 5. Treating the mode of a small sample as definitive.** With few data, a single change can move the mode. Detection: check the sample size before trusting the mode as a definitive conclusion. Correction: use the mode as exploration, not as conclusive proof, in small samples; complement it with the median.

**Error 6. Calculating the mode of categorical data as if they were numbers.** You cannot sum categories or sort them without an external criterion. Detection: if someone tries to compute the mean of colors, they are committing a category error. Correction: for unordered categorical data, only the mode makes sense; for ordered categorical data (satisfaction scale 1-5), the median also applies.

**Error 7. Rounding or adjusting the mode to make it a "round" value.** The mode is what it is: the most frequent value, even if it is 3.7 on a 1-to-5 scale if that is what appears most (in grouped or decimal discrete data). Do not force it to be an integer if the data are not. Detection: compare the reported mode with the actual frequencies. Correction: report the mode exactly as it emerges from the data.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The mode is the starting point for understanding distribution shape. A histogram with one clear peak is unimodal; with two peaks, bimodal; with several, multimodal. Each peak suggests a different subgroup in the data: for example, customers who buy little and customers who buy a lot, with few in the middle. Detecting bimodality with the mean would be impossible (the mean would fall in the valley between peaks, where almost nobody is); with the mode, it jumps out. That is the mode's most important contribution to exploratory analysis.

In descriptive statistics, the mode is used together with the mean and median to characterize skewness. In a perfectly symmetric, unimodal distribution, all three coincide. In a right-skewed distribution, typically mode < median < mean. That relationship is used as a quick diagnosis: if the three measures differ notably, the distribution is not symmetric and you should suspect outliers or mixed groups.

In probability, the mode of a discrete random variable is the value with the highest probability; for a continuous variable, it is the value where the density function reaches its maximum. It is the most probable point of the variable. That interpretation connects it with the idea of "most probable value", which is different from "average value" (mean) and "central value" (median).

In professional practice, the mode is standard in commerce (best-selling product), marketing (largest segment), public services (most frequent procedure), education (most common answer on an exam), and data science (dominant categorical variable in a dataset). When you read a report about consumer preferences and it only cites averages, be suspicious: for categorical preferences, the mode is the correct measure.

The most useful transfer is the habit of looking for peaks. Before summarizing a dataset with a single number, ask yourself whether there are clear peaks, whether several groups are mixed, or whether the data are uniformly spread. That question, more than the calculation itself, is what prevents you from summarizing badly data that actually contain several stories.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Compute the mode of: red, blue, red, green, blue, red. — **Solution:** red appears 3 times, blue 2, green 1. The mode is red.
2. Compute the mode of: 2, 3, 2, 1, 4, 2, 3, 2, 1, 5. — **Solution:** the 2 appears 4 times, more than anything else. The mode is 2.
3. The data are: 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4. How many modes are there and what are they? — **Solution:** 2 appears 2 times, 3 appears 2 times, 1 and 4 appear 1 time each. There are two modes: 2 and 3. The distribution is bimodal.
4. The data are: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. What is the mode? — **Solution:** each value appears exactly once. There is no mode.
5. A table of clothing sizes sold: S=10, M=45, L=30, XL=15. What is the modal size and what inventory decision does it suggest? — **Solution:** the modal size is M, with 45 sales. It suggests restocking M first, but you should also look at L (30) and S (10); do not base the entire purchase only on the mode because there is demand in other sizes.
6. Explain in two sentences why the mean and median cannot be computed for categorical data such as colors or brands. — **Solution:** The mean requires adding the values and dividing, and you cannot add colors or brands. The median requires sorting the values from smallest to largest, and there is no natural order among colors or brands. The mode only requires counting, so it is the only one of the three that applies.
**Map of five ideas.** First: the mode is the value or values that appear most frequently; it is computed by counting, without adding or sorting. Second: there can be one mode (unimodal), two (bimodal), several (multimodal), or none. Third: it is the only measure of central tendency valid for categorical data, where mean and median make no sense. Fourth: it is unstable in small samples; a single observation can create, eliminate or move it. Fifth: for numerical data, use it together with mean and median to detect peaks and skewness; never as the only summary measure.

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without notes why the data 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 have no mode, why a store should look at the mode of sizes before placing an order, and why the mode of a bimodal distribution tells you something the mean would hide, then you own this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, return to the matching example: the formula is a count, but the idea of "most frequent peak" is what stays.
