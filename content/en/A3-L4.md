## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute the weighted arithmetic mean of a dataset when each observation has a different weight, and understand why the weight changes the result compared with the simple mean. Second, compute the geometric mean of a list of positive values and know what it is for: growth rates, compounded returns, price indices. Third, compute the harmonic mean of a list of positive values and know what it is for: average speeds, price-quantity ratios, yields per unit. Fourth, prove and use the inequality of means for positive values: harmonic mean ≤ geometric mean ≤ arithmetic mean, and understand when equality holds. Fifth, decide in a concrete situation which mean is correct: arithmetic for sums, weighted for different importances, geometric for multiplicative factors, harmonic for ratios with a constant denominator. Sixth, detect the frequent mistake of computing the arithmetic mean of annual returns and show why it gives a misleading answer. Seventh, combine the weighted mean with the combined-means formula for groups of different sizes. Eighth, interpret the difference between the three means in a real case and explain what each one counts.

The prerequisites are those of the previous central tendency lessons, plus something new. You need to master the simple arithmetic mean, because all the others are variants of it. You need to handle powers and roots: the geometric mean uses the product of the values raised to 1/n, and the harmonic mean uses reciprocals. You need to understand what a weight is and why a value with more weight influences the result more. You need to know that all these means except the arithmetic and weighted ones are defined only for positive values (the arithmetic and weighted accept negatives but with care). And you should have read the lessons on mean, median and mode, because here we compare the three families of means.

A thirty-second entry check. Two investments: the first yields +10% and the second -10%. What is the average return? If you answered 0% (the arithmetic mean), you have the arithmetic answer but not the financial one: the final capital is 0.99 of the initial, a loss of 1%. If you answered something close to -0.5%, you already intuited the geometric mean. If you answered that there is not enough information because the order matters, you are right for compounded returns, but the geometric mean of the factors still gives a useful summary. This lesson exists precisely to separate those three answers.

A note on scope. The three means (weighted, geometric, harmonic) are summary tools, not causal calculators. They do not tell you why something happened; they tell you which value best represents a set under a specific weighting rule. Choosing the wrong mean is not a calculation error, it is an interpretation error: you answer a question that was not asked.

## 2. Why it matters

The simple arithmetic mean is the most famous, but it is not the one most used in real decisions about money and time. In finance, returns compound: if you gain 50% one year and lose 50% the next, you are not back to zero; you have lost 25% of your capital. The arithmetic mean would say 0%, which is false for your pocket. The geometric mean captures that loss. Without it, any investment performance report is misleading.

In logistics and transport, the average speed of a trip is not the arithmetic mean of the speeds of each leg if the distances are equal; it is the harmonic mean. A delivery driver who goes at 40 km/h and returns at 60 km/h does not travel at 50 km/h on average, but at 48 km/h. Using the arithmetic mean overestimates speed and underestimates delivery times, with real consequences for planning and costs.

In purchasing and budgeting, the weighted mean is what is used when values do not weigh equally. The average price of a supplier is not the simple mean of their invoices if some are for 10 units and others for 1,000; it is the weighted mean by quantity. Using the simple mean leads you to negotiate with a number that does not represent what you pay per unit.

It also matters because the inequality of means (harmonic ≤ geometric ≤ arithmetic) is a quick consistency check: if you compute the three means of the same set and they do not appear in that order, there is an error in at least one of the calculations. It is a safety net that costs nothing and prevents many reporting errors.

And there is a deeper reason. Each mean answers a different question about what it means to "represent" a set. The arithmetic answers "which value preserves the total sum?". The weighted answers "which value preserves the total weighted by importance?". The geometric answers "which constant factor gives the same final result when chained?". The harmonic answers "which constant speed covers the same distance in the same time?". Understanding that there is no generic "mean", but several answers to different questions, is what prevents using the wrong tool in each report.

## 3. Intuition

Start with the weighted mean. Imagine a professor gives two exams: one is worth 40% of the final grade and the other 60%. A student scores 6 on the first and 9 on the second. The final grade is not (6+9)/2 = 7.5, because the second exam weighs more. It is 6×0.4 + 9×0.6 = 2.4 + 5.4 = 7.8. The weighted mean is the simple mean when each datum has a different "weight" or importance, and that weight determines how much it moves the result. A datum with a large weight pulls the mean toward itself; one with a small weight barely touches it.

Now the geometric mean. Imagine an investment grows 10% the first year and 20% the second. The final capital is not the initial capital multiplied by (1+0.10+0.20)/2 = 1.15, because the percentages chain, they do not add. The final capital is initial × 1.10 × 1.20 = initial × 1.32. Which constant annual growth rate, applied twice, gives 1.32? It is the square root of 1.32, approximately 1.1489, that is, 14.89% per year. That is the meaning of the geometric mean: the constant factor that, multiplied by itself n times, gives the same final result as chaining the n real factors. It does not add percentages; it multiplies factors.

And the harmonic mean. Imagine a car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and another 60 km at 60 km/h. The total time is 60/30 + 60/60 = 2 + 1 = 3 hours. The total distance is 120 km. The average speed is 120/3 = 40 km/h. The arithmetic mean of 30 and 60 gives 45, which is false: the car spends twice as much time at 30 as at 60, and the average speed must weigh the slow leg more. The harmonic mean, 2/(1/30 + 1/60) = 2/(3/60) = 40, captures exactly that: it weighs by time, not by distance.

An image that unites the three. Think of a group of values as people stepping onto a scale. The arithmetic mean treats them all equally: each puts a weight of 1. The weighted mean gives them different weights: some carry more ballast than others. The geometric mean does not add weights; it multiplies factors, so it is the scale of what grows or decays by chaining. The harmonic mean does not add distances; it adds times, so it is the scale of what takes time to cover the same path. Each one is a scale with a different rule for deciding how much each value influences the result.

An extreme case. Values: 1, 1, 100. The arithmetic mean is 34. The geometric mean is (1×1×100)^(1/3) ≈ 4.64. The harmonic mean is 3/(1+1+0.01) ≈ 2.97. The three answers are different because they answer different questions. The arithmetic says "the total shared equally is 34 each". The geometric says "the constant growth factor is 4.64". The harmonic says "the constant rate that covers the same in the same time is 2.97". None is false; each answers something different. The error is using the arithmetic when the question is about growth or speed.

## 4. Definition and notation

**Weighted arithmetic mean.** For observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with weights $w_1, \dots, w_n$ (all positive, and at least one nonzero), the weighted mean is

$$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}$$

When all weights are equal (for example, $w_i = 1$), it reduces to the simple arithmetic mean. When the weights are frequencies $f_j$ of grouped values $x_j$, it reduces to the frequency-table formula seen earlier: $\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_j x_j}{\sum f_j}$. The weights represent importance, quantity, credits, population or any magnitude that makes one observation count more than another.

**Geometric mean.** For positive values $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the geometric mean is

$$G = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i\right)^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdot x_2 \cdots x_n}$$

It requires all $x_i$ to be strictly positive; if any is zero, the product is zero and the geometric mean is zero; if any is negative, the root may not be real. The geometric mean is the value that, multiplied by itself $n$ times, gives the same product as the original values.

**Harmonic mean.** For positive values $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the harmonic mean is

$$H = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{x_i}}$$

It is the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals. It requires positive values (reciprocals of zero do not exist). The harmonic mean is appropriate when averaging speeds, ratios or rates over a fixed quantity (distance, quantity, time).

**Inequality of means.** For positive values, it always holds

$$H \le G \le \bar{x}$$

with equality if and only if all values are equal. This inequality is a quick consistency check: if you compute the three and they do not appear in this order, there is an error.

Decision table — when to use each mean:

| Mean | Formula | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic | $\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$ | Total sum, additive data without outliers | Average height of a group |
| Weighted | $\frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$ | Values with different importance or size | Grade average by credits |
| Geometric | $\sqrt[n]{\prod x_i}$ | Chained factors, growth rates | Average annual return |
| Harmonic | $\frac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}$ | Speeds, ratios, constant denominator | Average speed of a round trip |

**Combined mean of groups.** If you have two groups with sizes $n_1, n_2$ and means $\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2$, the mean of the whole is

$$\bar{x}_{12} = \frac{n_1\bar{x}_1 + n_2\bar{x}_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

This is the weighted mean with group sizes as weights. It extends to $k$ groups by summing in numerator and denominator.

## 5. Derivation and step-by-step properties

**Weighted mean: why weights are group sizes.** The weighted mean arises from wanting to preserve the weighted total. If you want a value $c$ such that $\sum w_i c = \sum w_i x_i$, you solve $c = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$. It is the same reasoning as the simple mean, but the "total" you preserve is the weighted total, not the simple total. That is why weights can be credits, quantities, populations or any magnitude of importance.

**Geometric mean: derivation via logarithms.** The simplest way to understand the geometric mean is to take logarithms. Define $y_i = \ln x_i$. The arithmetic mean of the $y_i$ is $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum \ln x_i = \ln\left(\prod x_i\right)^{1/n}$. Therefore $G = e^{\bar{y}}$. The geometric mean is the arithmetic mean on a logarithmic scale, brought back to the original scale. That explains why it is appropriate for returns and growth rates: percentages add on a logarithmic scale (factors multiply), and the geometric mean returns the equivalent constant factor.

**Harmonic mean: derivation via reciprocals.** Define $y_i = 1/x_i$. The arithmetic mean of the $y_i$ is $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum \frac{1}{x_i}$. The harmonic mean is $H = 1/\bar{y}$. It is the arithmetic mean on a reciprocal scale, brought back. That explains why it is appropriate for speeds over equal distances: time is the reciprocal of speed, and the arithmetic mean of times gives the mean time, whose reciprocal is the mean speed.

**Inequality of means: schematic proof.** For two positive values $a, b$:

- Arithmetic vs geometric: $(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2 \ge 0$ implies $a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$, i.e., $\frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$.
- Geometric vs harmonic: apply AM-GM to $1/a$ and $1/b$: $\frac{1/a + 1/b}{2} \ge \sqrt{1/(ab)}$, i.e., $\frac{a+b}{2ab} \ge \frac{1}{\sqrt{ab}}$, which solves to $\sqrt{ab} \ge \frac{2}{1/a + 1/b}$, or $G \ge H$.

For $n$ values it extends by induction or by the same logarithm and convexity trick. Equality $H = G = A$ holds if and only if all values are equal.

**Invariance property.** The geometric mean of values transformed by a positive power is the power of the original geometric mean: $G(x_i^k) = G(x_i)^k$. The harmonic mean of reciprocals is the reciprocal of the arithmetic: $H(1/x_i) = 1/A(x_i)$. These properties make them useful when changing scale.

**Breakdown point.** The geometric and harmonic means break down at zero or negative values (they are not defined or give zero). The weighted and arithmetic means have breakdown point zero in the face of an extreme outlier: a single huge value shoots them up. None of the four is robust like the median; all are sensitive to extreme values, though in different ways.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Weighted mean with university credits

A student takes three courses: Mathematics (6 credits, grade 7), Statistics (4 credits, grade 9) and Programming (5 credits, grade 6). The credit-weighted mean is

$$\bar{x}_w = \frac{6\times7 + 4\times9 + 5\times6}{6+4+5} = \frac{42 + 36 + 30}{15} = \frac{108}{15} = 7.2$$

The simple mean would be $(7+9+6)/3 = 22/3 \approx 7.33$, which overstates because the low grade (6 in Programming) has more credits and should weigh more. The weighted mean gives 7.2, which better reflects the student's actual performance.

### Example 2. Geometric mean of compounded returns

An investment yields +10% the first year and -10% the second. The factors are 1.10 and 0.90. The arithmetic mean of the percentages gives 0%, suggesting no gain or loss. But the final capital is initial × 1.10 × 0.90 = initial × 0.99, a loss of 1%. The geometric mean of the factors is $\sqrt{1.10 \times 0.90} = \sqrt{0.99} \approx 0.99499$, that is, an average annual return of approximately -0.5%. Applied over two years: $0.99499^2 \approx 0.99$, which matches the final capital exactly. The geometric mean captures the real loss; the arithmetic hides it.

### Example 3. Common mistake: arithmetic mean of annual returns

A fund yields +50% in year 1 and -50% in year 2. The arithmetic mean is 0%, suggesting the capital is the same. But the final capital is initial × 1.5 × 0.5 = initial × 0.75, a loss of 25%. The geometric mean is $\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5} = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866$, that is, an average annual loss of 13.4%. Two years at -13.4% give $0.866^2 \approx 0.75$. The lesson: never average chained percentage returns arithmetically; use the geometric mean of the factors.

### Example 4. Harmonic mean of speeds

A car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and another 60 km at 60 km/h. Outbound time is 60/30 = 2 h; return time, 60/60 = 1 h. Total time: 3 h. Total distance: 120 km. Average speed: 120/3 = 40 km/h. The harmonic mean gives $2/(1/30 + 1/60) = 2/(3/60) = 2/(0.05) = 40$ km/h. The arithmetic mean would give 45 km/h, which is false because the car spends twice as much time at the slow speed. The harmonic mean weighs by time, not by distance.

### Example 5. Weighted mean combining groups of different sizes

Two factory shifts produce parts with mean error of 6.5 mm (shift A, 30 parts) and 8.1 mm (shift B, 50 parts). The mean error of the whole is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{30\times6.5 + 50\times8.1}{30+50} = \frac{195 + 405}{80} = \frac{600}{80} = 7.5 \text{ mm}$$

The simple mean of the two means would give 7.3 mm, which is incorrect because shift B has more parts and must weigh more. The weighted mean gives 7.5 mm, which is the real error per part of the whole.

### Example 6. Inequality of means with positive values

Values: 4 and 9. Arithmetic: $(4+9)/2 = 6.5$. Geometric: $\sqrt{4\times9} = \sqrt{36} = 6$. Harmonic: $2/(1/4 + 1/9) = 2/(13/36) = 72/13 \approx 5.54$. We check $5.54 \le 6 \le 6.5$: the inequality holds. Equality only occurs if both values are identical.

### Example 7. Harmonic mean of price-quantity ratios

You buy 100 units of a product at 2 €/unit and another 100 units of the same product at 4 €/unit. What is the average price per unit? Total spend is $100\times2 + 100\times4 = 600$ €, and total units are 200, so the real average price is $600/200 = 3$ €. The harmonic mean of 2 and 4 is $2/(1/2 + 1/4) = 2/(0.75) = 2.67$, which is not 3. Here the weighted arithmetic mean by quantity is correct (3 €), because what is constant is the quantity (100 units each), not the spend. The harmonic mean would be correct if the spend were constant (for example, if you spent 100 € on each purchase and bought different quantities). This shows that choosing the mean depends on what stays constant.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Averaging percentage returns arithmetically.** This is the most frequent error in finance and the most costly. If one year yields +50% and the next -50%, the arithmetic mean is 0%, but the final capital is 75% of the initial. Detection: whenever percentages chain year after year, the question is about compounded growth, not addition. Correction: use the geometric mean of the factors ($1+r$), not the arithmetic mean of the percentages.

**Error 2. Using the arithmetic mean of speeds when distances are equal.** If you travel at 30 km/h outbound and 60 km/h return on the same road, the average speed is not 45 km/h; it is 40 km/h. Detection: ask what is constant. If distance is constant, use the harmonic mean. If time is constant, the arithmetic mean is correct. Correction: identify what stays constant before choosing the mean.

**Error 3. Confusing weighted mean with simple mean when combining groups.** If you combine two groups of different sizes, averaging their simple means gives an incorrect result unless the groups are equal. Detection: always ask for the size of each group before combining means. Correction: use the combined formula (weighted by group sizes).

**Error 4. Applying geometric or harmonic means to negative values or zero.** The geometric mean of a zero value is zero, and of a negative value may not be real. The harmonic mean of zero is undefined. Detection: check that all values are strictly positive before using geometric or harmonic. Correction: if there are zeros or negatives, use the arithmetic or weighted mean, or transform the data (for example, work with $1+r$ in returns).

**Error 5. Forgetting that the inequality $H \le G \le A$ only holds for positive values.** With negative or mixed values, the order can break. Detection: verify the sign of the data before invoking the inequality. Correction: use the inequality only as a check on sets of positive values.

**Error 6. Believing there is a single, generic "mean".** Each mean answers a different question. Using the arithmetic for everything is like using a scale with one rule for all kinds of loads. Detection: before calculating, ask what each datum represents, what stays constant and what you want to preserve (sum, product, ratio). Correction: choose the mean according to the answers to those questions.

**Error 7. Rounding the geometric mean too early.** The geometric mean involves roots; rounding intermediates can deviate the final result by several tenths, especially with many values. Detection: compare with the calculation without rounding. Correction: keep decimals in intermediates and round only at the end.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The geometric mean appears in finance as the compound annual growth rate (CAGR when annualized), in price indices (Paasche and Laspeyres indices use weighted forms), in demographic growth and in any process where changes chain multiplicatively. Without the geometric mean, you cannot speak truthfully about long-term returns.

The harmonic mean appears in physics (average speed, resistance of resistors in parallel), in engineering (yields, efficiency ratios) and in finance (aggregated price-to-earnings ratio). It is the appropriate mean when the denominator is what is distributed constantly.

The weighted mean is the basis of consumer price indices (CPI), academic grade averages, market-cap-weighted stock indices and any average where observations are not equally important. It is also the general form of the combined mean of groups, which we use all the time when joining samples.

The inequality of means is a quick consistency check and appears in optimization, economics and statistics as a bound. If in a report the three means of a positive set do not appear in order $H \le G \le A$, there is a guaranteed calculation error.

The most useful transfer is the prior question: what stays constant? If it is the total or sum, use arithmetic or weighted. If it is the product or chaining, use geometric. If it is the denominator (distance, quantity, spend), use harmonic. That question chooses the tool before the calculation can go wrong.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. A student has grades 7 (6 credits), 9 (4 credits) and 6 (5 credits). What is their credit-weighted mean? — **Solution:** $(6\times7 + 4\times9 + 5\times6)/15 = 108/15 = 7.2$. The simple mean gives 7.33, which overstates because the low grade weighs more credits.
2. An investment yields +10% and then -10%. What is the real average annual return? — **Solution:** the factors are 1.10 and 0.90. The geometric mean is $\sqrt{1.10\times0.90} = \sqrt{0.99} \approx 0.995$, i.e. -0.5% per year. The arithmetic gives 0%, which is false because the final capital is 0.99 of the initial.
3. A car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and 60 km at 60 km/h. What is the average speed? — **Solution:** total time = 2 + 1 = 3 h; distance = 120 km; average speed = 40 km/h. The harmonic mean gives $2/(1/30+1/60) = 40$. The arithmetic gives 45, which is false.
4. For values 4 and 9, compute the three means and verify the inequality. — **Solution:** A = 6.5; G = 6; H ≈ 5.54. It holds $H \le G \le A$ (5.54 ≤ 6 ≤ 6.5).
5. Two groups have means 10 (n=20) and 15 (n=30). What is the mean of the whole? — **Solution:** $(20\times10 + 30\times15)/50 = (200+450)/50 = 650/50 = 13$. The simple mean of the means (12.5) is incorrect because the groups are not equal.
6. Explain in two sentences why you cannot use the arithmetic mean of annual returns to know the real performance of a multi-year investment. — **Solution:** Because returns chain by multiplying factors, not by adding percentages. The arithmetic mean ignores the chaining and can show 0% when there was actually a loss, as with +50% and -50%.
**Map of five ideas.** First: the weighted mean is the generalization of the simple mean where each datum has a weight; it is used when observations are not equally important. Second: the geometric mean is the constant factor that chains the same as the real factors; it is used for returns and compounded growth rates. Third: the harmonic mean is the constant rate that covers the same in the same time; it is used for speeds and ratios with a constant denominator. Fourth: for positive values $H \le G \le A$ holds, with equality only if all values are equal. Fifth: before choosing a mean, ask what stays constant and what you want to preserve; that decision, not the calculation, determines which mean is correct.
