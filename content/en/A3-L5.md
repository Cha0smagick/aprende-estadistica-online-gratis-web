## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, decide in a concrete case which measure of central tendency to use: arithmetic mean, median, mode, geometric mean, harmonic mean or weighted mean. Second, explain why the choice of measure is not a technical detail but changes the conclusion you draw from the same data. Third, identify whether a distribution is symmetric or skewed by comparing mean and median, and know what that implies for the choice of measure. Fourth, recognize when categorical data make only the mode meaningful and when numerical data with outliers make the median more honest than the mean. Fifth, compute and distinguish when to use the geometric mean (compounded returns, multiplicative factors) versus the arithmetic. Sixth, compute and distinguish when to use the harmonic mean (speeds, ratios with constant denominator) versus the arithmetic. Seventh, compute a weighted mean when observations have different weights and explain why the simple mean would be misleading. Eighth, always report mean and median together in a serious analysis, using the difference between them as a diagnosis of skewness.

The prerequisites are the three previous central tendency lessons: mean, median and mode, plus the lesson on weighted, geometric and harmonic means. You need to know how to compute each one, but above all you need to understand what question each one answers. You need to sort data, identify outliers and read a histogram at a very basic level. And you need to have worked with concrete examples (salaries, prices, speeds, returns) so the decision is not abstract.

A thirty-second entry check. You have the salaries of five employees: 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. Which measure would you use to say how much the typical employee earns? If you answered the median (24 thousand), you are ready. If you answered the mean (58.4 thousand), you fell into the outlier trap; this lesson exists to prevent it. If you answered the mode, there is no mode here (all values are distinct), so it does not apply. If you hesitated among several, review the median lesson before continuing.

A note on scope. This lesson does not give you a mechanical rule of the type "if this, use that mean". It gives you a decision framework: questions to ask before calculating. The golden rule is that there is no universal mean; there are several answers to different questions, and choosing the wrong one is answering a question that was not asked.

## 2. Why it matters

Choosing the wrong measure of central tendency is not a minor error; it is a decision that changes the conclusion and can lead to wrong decisions. Imagine a real estate report saying "the average house price in this neighborhood is 450,000 euros" when the median is 280,000 because a few luxury mansions pull the mean upward. A typical buyer who reads that report may think they cannot afford anything in the neighborhood, when in fact the typical house is much cheaper. The choice of measure distorted reality.

The same happens in human resources. If a company reports the mean salary and there is an executive earning ten times more than the rest, the mean rises and employees feel they earn below average when in fact most earn close to the median. Honest transparency requires reporting both: mean and median, and explaining the difference.

In finance, the mistake of using the arithmetic mean of annual returns instead of the geometric mean can make an investor believe their fund neither gained nor lost when in fact it lost 25% of its capital. In logistics, using the arithmetic mean of speeds instead of the harmonic mean can make a delivery company underestimate delivery times and promise impossible deadlines.

It also matters because the choice of measure reveals your intention. Reporting the mean when you care about the typical case says you are interested in the total or aggregate. Reporting the median when you care about the typical individual says you are interested in the person in the middle. Reporting the mode when you work with categories says you are interested in the most frequent. Each measure tells a different story about the same data, and choosing consciously which story to tell is part of being an honest analyst.

And there is a practical reason: in real life, most distributions are not symmetric. Salaries, house prices, waiting times, social media followers, insurance claims: almost everything has a long right tail. In those distributions, mean and median diverge, and choosing between them is not a detail; it is the center of the analysis.

## 3. Intuition

Think of measures of central tendency as different cameras pointed at the same data landscape. The arithmetic mean is a wide-angle lens that captures the total: it sums everything and shares it equally. It is honest with the total, but sensitive to extremes: one very rich person or one very expensive house appears in the image and displaces it.

The median is a telephoto lens focused on the middle: it sorts the data and looks at who is exactly in the center. It ignores how tall the tallest is or how short the shortest is; it only counts how many are on each side. It is honest with the typical case in skewed distributions, but it does not preserve the total: multiplying the median by the number of observations does not give the real sum.

The mode is a close-up camera looking for the most frequent peak: the color that sells most, the most common size, the most repeated answer. It does not add, it does not sort, it only counts. It is the only one that works with categories, but in numerical data it can be unstable or nonexistent.

The geometric mean is a time-lapse camera: it looks at how something grows year after year and tells you the constant factor that, applied repeatedly, gives the same final result. It is for returns, population growth, price indices.

The harmonic mean is a motion camera: it looks at how long it takes to cover equal distances at different speeds and tells you the constant speed that would cover the same in the same time. It is for speeds, ratios, yields.

The weighted mean is a selective-focus camera: it gives more importance to some data than others according to their size, credits or quantity. It is for when observations are not equally important.

The central intuition is this: before pointing the camera, ask what you want to see. If you want the total, use the mean (or the sum). If you want the typical case in a distribution with extremes, use the median. If you want the most frequent, use the mode. If you want compounded growth, use the geometric mean. If you want speed or ratios, use the harmonic mean. If you want different importance per observation, use the weighted mean.

An extreme case to fix the idea. Data: 1, 1, 1, 1, 100. The arithmetic mean is 20.8. The median is 1. The mode is 1. The geometric mean is (1×1×1×1×100)^(1/5) ≈ 2.51. The harmonic mean is 5/(1+1+1+1+0.01) ≈ 1.24. The six answers are different because they answer different questions. The arithmetic says "the total shared equally is 20.8 each". The median and mode say "the typical case is 1". The geometric says "the constant growth factor is 2.51". The harmonic says "the constant rate is 1.24". None is false; each answers something different. The error is using the arithmetic when what you want is to describe the typical case.

## 4. Definition and decision framework

There is no single formula that summarizes everything. There is a family of measures, each with its definition and purpose. Here we summarize them and give the framework for choosing.

**Simple arithmetic mean.** $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$. Use it when what matters is the total and the data are additive (heights, weights, scores, counts) and there are no extreme values dominating it. It is the default mean, but not the mean for everything.

**Median.** Middle ordered value. Use it when data are skewed or have outliers (salaries, house prices, waiting times) and you want to describe the typical case. It is robust: an outlier does not move it.

**Mode.** Most frequent value. Use it with categorical data (colors, brands, survey answers) and to detect peaks in distributions (bimodality, multimodality). It is the only one that works with non-orderable categories.

**Geometric mean.** $G = (\prod x_i)^{1/n}$. Use it with positive values when changes chain multiplicatively: annual returns, growth rates, price indices. Never average percentage returns arithmetically.

**Harmonic mean.** $H = \frac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}$. Use it with positive values when what stays constant is the numerator (distance, quantity, spend) and you average speeds or ratios. Never average speeds with equal distances using the arithmetic mean.

**Weighted mean.** $\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$. Use it when observations have different weights (credits, quantities, population, importance). It is the generalization of the simple mean and the basis of weighted indices.

**Decision framework (prior questions).** Before calculating, answer three things:
1. What does each number represent and in what unit? (Is it money, time, distance, a category?)
2. What stays constant? (Distance, spend, time, or nothing?)
3. What do I want to preserve or describe? (The total, the typical case, the most frequent, compounded growth, constant rate?)

The answers choose the measure. No exceptions: calculating first and deciding after is putting the cart before the horse.

## 5. Step-by-step choice guide


When to use each measure — quick decision table:

| Measure | Formula | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic mean | $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$ | Total sum, additive data without outliers | Average height of a group |
| Median | Middle ordered value | Skewed data, outliers | Typical salary |
| Mode | Most frequent value | Categorical data, detecting peaks | Best-selling color |
| Geometric mean | $G = \sqrt[n]{\prod x_i}$ | Chained factors, growth rates | Average annual return |
| Harmonic mean | $H = \frac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}$ | Speeds, ratios, constant denominator | Average speed of a round trip |
| Weighted mean | $\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$ | Observations with different weights | Grade average by credits |

Inequality of means (positive values):

$$H \le G \le \bar{x}$$

with equality if and only if all values are equal.


**Step 1. Are the data categorical?** If they cannot be ordered or added (colors, brands, cities), the only valid measure is the mode. Stop there: do not compute mean or median.

**Step 2. Are the data numerical?** Proceed to the next step.

**Step 3. Are there outliers or skewness?** Compare mean and median. If they differ a lot, the distribution is skewed and the mean does not describe the typical case. Use the median for the typical case and the mean only if you care about the total.

**Step 4. Do you care about the total or aggregate?** If yes (budget, total spend, total income), use the arithmetic mean (or the weighted mean if there are weights). The median does not preserve the total.

**Step 5. Do changes chain multiplicatively?** If yes (returns, growth, indices), use the geometric mean of the factors, not the arithmetic mean of percentages.

**Step 6. Are you averaging speeds or ratios over a fixed quantity?** If yes (round-trip average speed, price per unit with constant spend), use the harmonic mean.

**Step 7. Do observations have different weights?** If yes (credits, quantities, population), use the weighted mean. The simple mean would treat all equally and be misleading.

**Step 8. Do you want a quick diagnosis?** Report mean and median together. If they are similar, the distribution is reasonably symmetric. If they differ a lot, there is skewness and outliers; explain which one you use and why.

This flow is not a mechanical recipe; it is a checklist that forces you to think before calculating.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Skewed salaries: mean vs median

Five employees earn 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. The mean is 58.4 thousand; the median is 24 thousand. If the report says "the average salary is 58.4 thousand", the reader thinks the typical employee earns that, which is false: four of five earn less than half. The median (24 thousand) better describes the typical employee. The mean only serves if what interests you is the total payroll (5 × 58.4 = 292 thousand). Conclusion: report both and say which one you use for what.

### Example 2. Compounded returns: geometric vs arithmetic

An investment yields +50% in year 1 and -50% in year 2. The arithmetic mean is 0%, suggesting the capital is the same. But the final capital is initial × 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 of initial: a loss of 25%. The geometric mean is $\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5} = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866$, i.e., an average annual loss of 13.4%. Two years at -13.4% give approximately 0.75. Conclusion: never average chained percentage returns arithmetically; use the geometric mean of the factors.

### Example 3. Average speed: harmonic vs arithmetic

A car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and 60 km at 60 km/h. The arithmetic mean gives 45 km/h, false. Total time is 2 + 1 = 3 h; distance, 120 km; average speed, 40 km/h. The harmonic mean gives $2/(1/30 + 1/60) = 40$ km/h. Conclusion: for speeds with constant distance, use the harmonic mean; for constant times, the arithmetic mean.

### Example 4. Categorical data: only the mode

A survey of 20 people about their favorite color gives red 7 times, blue 8 times, green 5 times. The mode is blue. There is no mean or median of colors: they cannot be added or ordered. Conclusion: with categories, only the mode applies.

### Example 5. Combining groups of different sizes: weighted vs simple

Two shifts produce parts with mean error 6.5 mm (30 parts) and 8.1 mm (50 parts). The simple mean of the means gives 7.3 mm, incorrect. The weighted mean gives $(30\times6.5 + 50\times8.1)/80 = 7.5$ mm, which is the real error per part of the whole. Conclusion: when combining means of unequal groups, weight by sizes.

### Example 6. Diagnosis with mean and median

Prices of ten houses are: 150,000 to 230,000 plus one at 2,000,000 euros. The mean is 371,000; the median is 205,000. The large difference indicates strong skewness (one mansion pulls the mean). To describe the typical house, use the median (205,000). For the total market value, use the mean (or the sum). Conclusion: the divergence between mean and median is a warning signal, not a detail.

### Example 7. Weighted mean in purchasing

A supplier charges you 2 € per unit for an order of 100 units and 4 € per unit for another order of 100 units. The quantity-weighted mean price is $(100\times2 + 100\times4)/200 = 3$ €. If you used the simple mean of the unit prices (2 and 4), it would also give 3 € because quantities are equal; but if orders were 10 and 100 units, the simple mean would give 3 € and the weighted mean would give $(10\times2 + 100\times4)/110 \approx 3.82$ €, which is the real price you pay per unit. Conclusion: use the weighted mean when quantities differ.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Using the mean to describe the typical case in skewed distributions.** This is the most frequent and most damaging error. A mean salary of 58.4 thousand in a group where four of five earn less than 30 thousand misleads the reader. Detection: compare mean and median; if they differ a lot, do not use the mean alone for the typical case. Correction: report the median for the typical and the mean only for the total, and say which is which.

**Error 2. Averaging percentage returns arithmetically.** +50% and -50% give arithmetic mean 0%, but final capital is 0.75 of initial. Detection: if percentages chain year after year, the question is compounded. Correction: geometric mean of the factors ($1+r$).

**Error 3. Averaging speeds arithmetically with equal distances.** 30 km/h outbound and 60 km/h return give arithmetic mean 45, but real is 40. Detection: if distance is constant, use the harmonic mean. Correction: identify what stays constant.

**Error 4. Combining means of groups of different sizes with the simple mean.** Only correct if groups are equal. Detection: ask for the size of each group. Correction: weighted mean by sizes.

**Error 5. Computing mean or median of categorical data.** You cannot add colors or order brands. Detection: if data are unordered categories, only the mode applies. Correction: use the mode; do not force mean or median.

**Error 6. Believing one measure serves for everything.** There is no generic "mean". Each answers a different question. Detection: if you always use the arithmetic without asking what you want to preserve, you are committing this error. Correction: apply the decision framework of section 4 before calculating.

**Error 7. Reporting only one measure when two are appropriate.** Reporting only the mean hides skewness; reporting only the median hides the total. Detection: in serious reports, one of the two is missing. Correction: report mean and median together and explain the difference.

**Error 8. Rounding before finishing.** Especially in geometric and harmonic means, rounding intermediates deviates the result. Detection: compare with calculation without rounding. Correction: keep decimals and round only at the end.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The choice of central tendency measure connects with the rest of the course. The median is the basis of the interquartile range and boxplots; without understanding when to use the median, you do not understand outlier detection by IQR. The mean is the basis of variance, standard deviation, standard error and confidence intervals; without knowing when to use the mean, you do not understand inference. The geometric mean connects with compounded returns and CAGR in finance; the harmonic mean with physics (resistors in parallel, average speed); the weighted mean with price indices (CPI) and market-cap-weighted stock indices.

In professional practice, the choice of measure is a communication decision as much as a calculation one. A report saying "the average price is X" when the median is very different is choosing a narrative. An honest analyst reports both and explains why they prefer one in each context. That transparency is what separates rigorous analysis from selectively using numbers to support a preconceived conclusion.

The most useful transfer is the habit of the prior question. Before opening Excel or writing a formula, ask yourself: what do I want to say with this number? The total, the typical, the most frequent, the growth, the rate? That question, more than the calculation, is what prevents summarizing badly data that contain several stories.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Five employees earn 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. Which measure best describes the typical employee and which is used for total payroll? — **Solution:** The median (24 thousand) describes the typical; the mean (58.4 thousand) is used for total payroll (5 × 58.4 = 292 thousand).
2. An investment yields +50% and then -50%. Which mean gives the real return and what is it? — **Solution:** The geometric mean of the factors: $\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5} = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866$, i.e., an average annual loss of 13.4%. The arithmetic gives 0%, false.
3. A car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and 60 km at 60 km/h. What is the real average speed and which mean gives 45 erroneously? — **Solution:** The real one is 40 km/h (harmonic). The arithmetic gives 45 erroneously because it ignores that more time is spent at the slow speed.
4. A survey gives red 7 times, blue 8 times, green 5 times. Which is the only valid measure? — **Solution:** The mode (blue). You cannot compute mean or median of colors.
5. Two groups have means 10 (n=20) and 15 (n=30). What is the mean of the whole? — **Solution:** $(20\times10 + 30\times15)/50 = (200+450)/50 = 650/50 = 13$. The simple mean of the means (12.5) is incorrect because groups differ in size.
6. Prices of ten houses range from 150,000 to 230,000 plus one at 2,000,000. The mean is 371,000 and the median 205,000. What does the large difference indicate and which would you use for the typical house? — **Solution:** It indicates strong skewness (outlier). For the typical house, use the median (205,000).
**Map of five ideas.** First: there is no universal mean; each answers a different question (total, typical, frequent, growth, rate, importance). Second: before calculating, ask what each datum represents, what stays constant and what you want to preserve. Third: in skewed distributions, report mean and median together; the difference between them diagnoses skewness. Fourth: with categorical data, only the mode applies. Fifth: choosing the wrong mean is not a calculation error; it is answering the wrong question.

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without notes why a mean salary of 58.4 thousand can mislead when the median is 24 thousand, why +50% and -50% are not 0% real return, and why the average speed of a round trip is not the arithmetic mean of the speeds, then you own this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, return to the matching example: the decision of which measure to use is more important than the calculation itself.
