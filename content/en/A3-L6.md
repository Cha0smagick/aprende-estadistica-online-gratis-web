## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, recognize in a histogram or dataset whether the distribution is symmetric or skewed, and toward which side the tail points. Second, compare mean, median and mode on the same dataset and say what ordering among them indicates right skew, left skew or symmetry. Third, compute and interpret Pearson's skewness coefficient $g_1 = 3(\bar{x} - \tilde{x})/s$ and know what its sign and magnitude mean. Fourth, explain why mean ≈ median ≈ mode in a symmetric distribution and why they diverge in a skewed one. Fifth, decide which measure of central tendency to report according to the shape of the distribution, not according to habit. Sixth, detect the frequent mistake of assuming that skewness means "something wrong" or "erroneous data", when it is only a legitimate distribution shape. Seventh, connect the mean-median relationship with the choice of measure from the previous lesson and with outlier detection. Eighth, interpret in a real case (salaries, house prices, waiting times) what each measure counts and which one is honest.

The prerequisites are the previous central tendency lessons: mean, median, mode, and the lesson on when to use each one. You need to know how to compute the standard deviation (or at least understand what it measures: dispersion around the mean), because Pearson's coefficient uses it in the denominator. You need to read a histogram at a basic level: identify the peak, the tail and the center. And you should have worked with examples of skewed distributions (salaries, house prices) so the intuition is not abstract.

A thirty-second entry check. Data: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 100. Sorted already. The median is (2+3)/2 = 2.5. The mean is (1+2+2+3+4+100)/6 = 112/6 ≈ 18.67. The mode is 2. What does the ordering mode < median < mean tell you? If you answered "right skew, tail toward high values", you are ready. If you answered "the mean is wrong because 100 is an error", you have confused an outlier with a data entry error; this lesson helps you distinguish them. If you answered they are equal, review the median lesson before continuing.

A note on scope. This lesson does not aim to prove advanced skewness theorems; it aims to give you a quick diagnostic tool you can use in any report. The mean-median-mode relationship is a compass, not a definitive sentence: there are skewed distributions where mean and median are close, and symmetric distributions where the mode differs. Use it as a clue, not as proof.

## 2. Why it matters

The relationship between mean, median and mode is one of the most underestimated diagnostic tools in descriptive statistics. It lets you look at a dataset and say, in seconds, whether the distribution is symmetric or has a long tail, and toward which side. That is not an academic curiosity: it changes the conclusion you draw and the measure you report.

Imagine a real estate report saying "the average house price is 450,000 euros" without saying anything else. If the median is 280,000 because a few mansions pull the mean upward, the typical buyer gets a false picture of the market. The relationship mean > median warns you that there is a right tail and that the mean is not the typical case. Without that comparison, the report is incomplete and potentially misleading.

The same happens in public services. If the mean waiting time in the emergency room is 45 minutes but the median is 25, the tail of patients waiting hours is pulling the mean upward. Managing with the mean would lead you to size resources for a typical patient who does not exist; managing with the median gives a more honest picture of the majority's experience.

In finance, asset returns often have left tails (occasional extreme losses) or right tails (exceptional gains). The relationship between mean and median warns you whether the return distribution is symmetric or whether there is tail risk that the mean hides.

It also matters because the mean-median-mode relationship is the first signal that something is not a perfect bell curve. Many statistical techniques (t-tests, confidence intervals, regression) assume normality or at least approximate symmetry. If mean and median diverge a lot, that assumption is at risk and you need robust or non-parametric methods.

And there is a deeper conceptual reason. The mean, the median and the mode answer three different questions about the center: which value preserves the total?, which occupies the middle?, which is the most frequent?. When they coincide, the center is clear and univocal. When they diverge, they are telling you that the data do not have a single obvious center, but several faces. That divergence is information, not noise.

## 3. Intuition

Picture a line of people ordered by height, from shortest to tallest. The median is the height of the person in the middle. The mode is the height that repeats most often. The mean is the height that, if everyone had it, would preserve the same total sum of heights.

In a symmetric distribution —picture a perfect bell curve—, the middle person, the most frequent value and the value that balances the total coincide. The distribution looks the same to the left and to the right of the center, so mean = median = mode. There is no tail pulling toward either side.

Now imagine a few people are extremely tall (basketball players, for example). The median barely moves: it is still the middle person, because only a few are very tall and they do not change the count of how many are on each side. The mode does not move either: it remains the most common height among the majority. But the mean does move: those very tall people add a lot to the total and pull it upward. Result: mode < median < mean. The right tail (the high values) pulls the mean up.

Conversely, imagine a few people are extremely short. The mean is dragged downward, while the median and mode stay put. Result: mean < median < mode. The left tail pulls the mean down.

An extreme case to fix the idea. Data: 20, 22, 24, 26, 200 (salaries in thousands of euros). The median is 24, there is no mode (all distinct), the mean is 58.4. Here median < mean, and the difference is large. That tells you two things: the distribution has a right tail (the 200 salary) and the mean does not describe the typical employee. If a report uses the mean alone, it is telling the story of the total, not the story of the typical person.

The central intuition is this: the mean is sensitive to the magnitude of values; the median only counts how many are on each side; the mode only counts how many times each repeats. When the three diverge, each is telling a different story about the same data. Your job is to decide which story is relevant for your decision.

## 4. Definition and notation

Let a sample of $n$ observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ have mean $\bar{x}$, median $\tilde{x}$ and mode (or modes) $m$. The relationship among them is summarized in three cases:

**Symmetric, unimodal distribution:**
$$\bar{x} \approx \tilde{x} \approx m$$
The distribution looks the same on both sides of the center. Mean, median and mode fall at the same point (or very close).

**Right-skewed distribution (right tail, rare high values):**
$$\bar{x} > \tilde{x} \gtrapprox m$$
The mean is greater than the median because the high values in the tail add a lot and pull it upward. The median and mode stay closer to the bulk of the data.

**Left-skewed distribution (left tail, rare low values):**
$$\bar{x} < \tilde{x} \ltrapprox m$$
The mean is less than the median because the low values in the tail subtract a lot and pull it downward.

The symbols $\gtrapprox$ and $\ltrapprox$ indicate "generally greater/less than", not a strict law: there are exceptions, especially in small samples or multimodal distributions.

**Pearson's skewness coefficient (second form):**
$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{\bar{x} - \tilde{x}}{s}$$
where $s$ is the sample standard deviation. The factor 3 makes the coefficient close to zero when mean and median coincide in an approximately normal distribution. The sign of $g_1$ indicates the direction of skewness: positive for right tail, negative for left tail. The magnitude indicates how strong the skewness is relative to dispersion.

The elements of the definition, one by one. $\bar{x}$ is the arithmetic mean. $\tilde{x}$ is the median. $m$ is the mode (if there are several, take the main one or report that there are several). $s$ is the standard deviation: $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2}$. The numerator $\bar{x} - \tilde{x}$ measures how far apart mean and median are; dividing by $s$ normalizes that separation by the data dispersion, so the coefficient is comparable across datasets with different units.

Two limiting cases. If all values are equal, mean = median = mode and $s = 0$, so the coefficient is 0/0 (indeterminate, but the distribution is clearly symmetric). If $n$ is very small, the mean-median-mode relationship can be unstable: one data point can move the mean and change the sign of $g_1$.

A precision: Pearson's coefficient is a quick approximation, not a definitive proof of skewness. For small samples or multimodal distributions, complement it with a histogram and with the moment skewness coefficient ($\frac{1}{n}\sum((x_i-\bar{x})/s)^3$), which is more stable but more complex.

## 5. Step-by-step interpretation of Pearson's coefficient

The coefficient $g_1 = 3(\bar{x} - \tilde{x})/s$ is interpreted as follows:


Summary of the mean-median-mode relationship:

| Distribution type | Relationship | What it indicates | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetric unimodal | mean ≈ median ≈ mode | No dominant tail | Adult heights |
| Right-skewed | mean > median ≳ mode | Tail toward high values | Salaries, house prices |
| Left-skewed | mean < median ≲ mode | Tail toward low values | Retirement ages, easy exams |


**Step 1. Compute mean, median and standard deviation.** You need all three. The median requires sorting; the standard deviation requires the mean and squared deviations.

**Step 2. Compute the numerator $\bar{x} - \tilde{x}$.** If positive, the mean is above the median: right tail. If negative, the mean is below the median: left tail. If zero (or very close), no skewness detected by this measure.

**Step 3. Divide by $s$ and multiply by 3.** This normalizes the difference by dispersion. A difference of 2 units is large if data range from 0 to 5, but small if they range from 0 to 1000. Dividing by $s$ puts everything on the same scale.

**Step 4. Interpret the sign.** $g_1 > 0$: right skew (long tail toward high values). Typical of salaries, house prices, waiting times. $g_1 < 0$: left skew (long tail toward low values). Typical of retirement ages, easy exam scores (many score high, few score very low). $g_1 \approx 0$: no skewness detected by mean-median.

**Step 5. Interpret the magnitude.** There are no universal thresholds, but as a practical rule: $|g_1| < 0.5$ indicates light or no skewness; $0.5 \le |g_1| < 1$ indicates moderate skewness; $|g_1| \ge 1$ indicates strong skewness. These values are orientative; always complement with a histogram.

**Step 6. Connect with the choice of measure.** If $g_1$ indicates strong right skew, report the median as the typical case and the mean only if you care about the total. If $g_1 \approx 0$, mean and median are interchangeable for describing the center.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Salaries: right skew

Five employees earn 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. The mean is 58.4; the median is 24; there is no mode. The standard deviation is high because of the 200. Pearson's coefficient is

$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{58.4 - 24}{s}$$

with $s$ high, so $g_1$ is positive and large. This confirms right skew: the tail of the 200 salary pulls the mean upward. The median (24) describes the typical employee; the mean (58.4) describes the total payroll. A report using only the mean is telling the story of the total, not of the typical person.

### Example 2. Symmetric distribution

Data: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. The mean is 6, the median is 6, there is no mode (all distinct) or we could say the center is 6. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{\frac{(2-6)^2+(4-6)^2+(6-6)^2+(8-6)^2+(10-6)^2}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{16+4+0+4+16}{4}} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$. The coefficient is $g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{6-6}{3.16} = 0$. No skewness. Mean, median and center coincide.

### Example 3. Left skew: retirement ages

The retirement ages of ten people are: 60, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69 and 55 (one person retired early). Sorted: 55, 60, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69. The median is $(64+65)/2 = 64.5$. The mean is $(55+60+62+63+64+65+66+67+68+69)/10 = 639/10 = 63.9$. Here mean < median (63.9 < 64.5), signaling left skew: the low tail of 55 pulls the mean downward. The median still describes the typical retirement age better.

### Example 4. Computing $g_1$ with numbers

Data: 10, 12, 12, 14, 16, 100. Mean: $(10+12+12+14+16+100)/6 = 164/6 \approx 27.33$. Median: $(12+14)/2 = 13$. Mode: 12. Standard deviation: first squared deviations: $(10-27.33)^2 \approx 299.6$, $(12-27.33)^2 \approx 235.0$, $(12-27.33)^2 \approx 235.0$, $(14-27.33)^2 \approx 177.7$, $(16-27.33)^2 \approx 128.4$, $(100-27.33)^2 \approx 5288.4$. Sum $\approx 6364.1$. Divided by $n-1=5$: $1272.8$. Root: $s \approx 35.68$. Coefficient: $g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{27.33 - 13}{35.68} = 3 \cdot \frac{14.33}{35.68} \approx 3 \cdot 0.4016 \approx 1.20$. Positive and above 1: strong right skew, driven by the 100.

### Example 5. Mode in skewed distribution

Data: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 100. Mean: $(1+2+2+3+4+5+100)/7 = 117/7 \approx 16.71$. Median: 3 (middle value of 7). Mode: 2. Order: mode (2) < median (3) < mean (16.71). Clear right skew. The mode is at the low peak, the median in the middle, and the mean pulled by the 100.

### Example 6. Misinterpretation to avoid

A report says "skewness is 1.20, so the data are wrong". That is false: skewness measures distribution shape, not data quality. Salaries are naturally skewed because a few people earn much more. Skewness is not an error; it is a property of the data you must respect when choosing the measure and the analysis method.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Assuming skewness means "wrong data" or "something bad".** Skewness is a legitimate distribution shape. Salaries, house prices and waiting times are naturally skewed. Detection: if someone says "the data are wrong because mean and median differ", they are confusing shape with quality. Correction: report skewness as a characteristic, not a defect, and choose the measure accordingly.

**Error 2. Using the mean alone to describe the typical case in a skewed distribution.** We saw the salary example: mean 58.4 vs median 24. Detection: if a report cites only the mean and you know there are outliers, ask for the median. Correction: report both and explain the difference.

**Error 3. Believing mean = median = mode always in real data.** That equality only holds in perfectly symmetric distributions, which rarely exist in real life. Detection: if someone assumes equality without checking, they are ignoring the shape of the data. Correction: always compare the three before reporting a single one.

**Error 4. Interpreting the sign of $g_1$ backwards.** $g_1 > 0$ is right tail (rare high values), not left tail. Detection: remember that the mean is pulled toward the tail, so if $g_1 > 0$, the mean is above the median and the tail is upward. Correction: visualize the histogram before interpreting the sign.

**Error 5. Using $g_1$ as definitive proof of normality.** $g_1 \approx 0$ does not prove data are normal; it only says mean and median are close. There are symmetric but non-normal distributions (e.g., symmetric bimodal). Detection: if someone says "since $g_1 \approx 0$, data are normal", they are overstating. Correction: complement with histograms, normality tests and context knowledge.

**Error 6. Ignoring the mode in the comparison.** The mode can be far from both mean and median in multimodal distributions. Detection: if you report only mean and median and there are two clear peaks, you are omitting information. Correction: look at the histogram and report the mode if there are relevant peaks.

**Error 7. Rounding $g_1$ too early.** The coefficient depends on the difference between mean and median, which can be small. Rounding intermediates can change the sign. Detection: keep decimals in mean, median and standard deviation. Correction: calculate with precision and round only at the end.

**Error 8. Believing a large $|g_1|$ invalidates all parametric techniques.** Strong skew suggests caution, but does not automatically invalidate everything. It depends on sample size and technique. Detection: if someone says "since $g_1 = 2$, nothing parametric can be done", that is excessive. Correction: evaluate case by case; consider transformations (logarithm for right tail) or robust methods.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The mean-median-mode relationship connects directly with the previous lesson on when to use each measure. If $g_1$ indicates right skew, the median is the appropriate measure for the typical case; if $g_1 \approx 0$, mean and median are equivalent. That connection makes Pearson's coefficient not an isolated number, but a guide for decision-making.

It also connects with statistical inference. Many parametric techniques (t-tests, confidence intervals, ANOVA, linear regression) assume errors or data are approximately normal, which implies symmetry. If $g_1$ indicates strong skew, those assumptions are at risk and you should consider robust methods (median, non-parametric tests) or transforming the data (logarithm for right tail).

In probability, skewness is a third-order moment of the distribution. Pearson's coefficient is a simplified version of the moment skewness coefficient ($\frac{1}{n}\sum((x_i-\bar{x})/s)^3$), more stable but more complex to compute by hand. Both measure the same thing: the degree and direction of deviation from symmetry.

In professional practice, the mean-median relationship is used as a quick diagnostic in business, health and public policy reports. Before presenting a mean, the serious analyst compares it with the median and, if they differ, explains why they use one or the other. That transparency is what separates rigorous analysis from selectively using figures to support a preconceived conclusion.

The most useful transfer is the habit of the mental histogram. Before calculating any summary, imagine the shape of the data: is there a long tail toward some side? Are there several peaks? That image tells you which measure to choose before the calculation can mislead you.
