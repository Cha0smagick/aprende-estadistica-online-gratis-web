## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute the coefficient of variation $CV = s/\bar{x}$ (often expressed as a percentage by multiplying by 100) of a dataset. Second, explain in your own words why the CV is dimensionless and what that means: it allows comparing dispersion between variables with different units or with very different means. Third, interpret the CV: a high CV indicates more relative variability; a low CV, more relative homogeneity. Fourth, decide when to use the CV instead of the absolute standard deviation: use the CV when comparing dispersion across variables with different units (cm vs kg) or with very different means. Fifth, detect the frequent mistake of comparing standard deviations of variables with different units or disparate means without using the CV. Sixth, compute the standard deviation from the CV and the mean ($s = CV \times \bar{x}$). Seventh, recognize the limits of the CV: it breaks down when the mean is close to zero (the CV explodes) and it does not make sense with data that are not positive. Eighth, connect the CV with the concept of relative variability versus absolute variability.

The prerequisites are the previous dispersion lessons: range, IQR, variance and standard deviation. You need to know how to compute the mean and the standard deviation. You need to understand what a proportion or percentage is, because the CV is often expressed as %. And you should have seen examples of variables with different units (height in cm, weight in kg, price in euros) so that the idea of comparing dispersion across different units makes sense.

A thirty-second entry check. A group's mean height is 170 cm with standard deviation 5 cm. What is the CV? If you answered $5/170 \approx 0.029$ (or 2.9 %), you are ready. Mean weight is 70 kg with standard deviation 8 kg. What is the CV? If you answered $8/70 \approx 0.114$ (or 11.4 %), you are ready and already see that weight is relatively more variable than height, even though the absolute standard deviation of weight (8) is larger than that of height (5). If you answered that weight is "more variable just because 8 > 5", you have not understood the CV: you must compare relative to the mean. If you hesitated, review the standard deviation before continuing.

A note on scope. The CV is a relative comparison tool, not an absolute measure of dispersion. It does not tell you how much data vary in real units; it tells you how much they vary relative to their mean. Use it to compare, not to describe a single isolated dataset without context.

## 2. Why it matters

The absolute standard deviation has a problem: it depends on units and on the magnitude of the data. You cannot compare the standard deviation of height (in cm) with that of weight (in kg), nor the standard deviation of house prices (in thousands of euros) with that of egg prices (in cents). The CV solves that: by dividing the standard deviation by the mean, units cancel and you get a pure number that allows comparing relative variability across any pair of variables.

It also matters because the absolute standard deviation can mislead when means are very different. Two companies may have a sales standard deviation of 10,000 euros, but if one averages 100,000 euros and the other 1,000,000, the relative variability is very different: 10 % of the mean in the first case and only 1 % in the second. The CV captures that; the absolute standard deviation does not.

It also matters in fields where comparing consistency across processes is key: quality control (which production line is more stable?), finance (which asset has more risk relative to its expected return?), sports (which athlete is more consistent?). In all these cases, the CV is the right tool because it compares dispersion relative to the level of each variable.

And there is a practical reason. The CV tells you whether a standard deviation is "large" or "small" relative to the size of the data. A standard deviation of 5 cm is large if the mean is 10 cm (small children) and small if the mean is 180 cm (basketball players). The CV gives you that relative judgment automatically.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine two clothing stores. Store A sells an average of 100 t-shirts per day with a standard deviation of 20. Store B sells an average of 1,000 t-shirts per day with a standard deviation of 50. Which store is more irregular? If you look only at the standard deviation, you would say B is more irregular (50 > 20). But look at the CV: store A has CV = 20/100 = 0.20 (20 %); store B has CV = 50/1,000 = 0.05 (5 %). In relation to its size, store A is four times more irregular than B. The absolute standard deviation misled you because it did not take into account the sales level of each store.

That is the central intuition of the CV: the absolute standard deviation measures how much data vary in real units, but the CV measures how much they vary relative to their size. It is the difference between "they vary by 50 units" and "they vary by 5 % of their size".

An extreme case. A variable has a mean close to zero and a standard deviation of 1. The CV is 1/almost zero = a huge number. The CV "explodes" when the mean approaches zero, because dividing by a very small number inflates the result. That does not mean the data are infinitely variable; it means the CV is not useful when the mean is close to zero. Another limit: the CV does not make sense with data that can be negative or zero (temperatures in degrees Celsius, for example, because zero does not mean "absence of temperature").

Finally, the CV is dimensionless. That means it carries no units: it is not "cm" nor "kg", it is a pure number (or a percentage). That is what allows comparing the variability of heights (cm) with that of weights (kg) or of prices (euros) with that of ages (years).

## 4. Definition and notation

**Coefficient of variation.** For a sample with mean $\bar{x}$ and standard deviation $s$, the coefficient of variation is

$$CV = \frac{s}{\bar{x}}$$

often expressed as a percentage:

$$CV\% = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100$$

For a population, use $\sigma/\mu$ instead of $s/\bar{x}$.

**Interpretation.** The CV measures relative variability: how much data disperse in proportion to their mean. A low CV indicates that data are concentrated near the mean in proportion to the size of this mean; a high CV indicates high relative dispersion.

**When to use the CV.** Use the CV when you want to compare the dispersion of two or more variables that:
1. have different units (cm vs kg, euros vs years), or
2. have very different means even if they use the same unit.

Do not use the CV to describe a single isolated dataset (use the standard deviation for that) or when the mean is close to zero.



When to use each measure — quick decision table:

| Situation | Appropriate measure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Compare dispersion of variables with different units (cm vs kg) | Coefficient of variation (CV) | Dimensionless; units cancel |
| Compare dispersion of variables with same units but very different means | Coefficient of variation (CV) | Puts standard deviation in relation to the mean |
| Describe dispersion of a single dataset in real units | Standard deviation (s or σ) | Has the same units as the data |
| Mean close to zero or data with negatives | Standard deviation or IQR | The CV explodes or makes no sense |

**Recovering the standard deviation.** If you know the CV and the mean, you can obtain the standard deviation:

$$s = CV \times \bar{x}$$

**Limits of the CV.** The CV is not defined if $\bar{x} = 0$ (division by zero). It becomes unstable if $\bar{x}$ is very close to zero. It makes no sense with variables that take negative or zero values significantly (temperature in Celsius, profits that can be negative). For those variables, use the absolute standard deviation or the IQR.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Compute the mean.** $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$.

**Step 2. Compute the standard deviation.** $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}$ (sample) or $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}$ (population).

**Step 3. Compute the CV.** Divide the standard deviation by the mean: $CV = s/\bar{x}$.

**Step 4. Express as a percentage (optional).** Multiply by 100: $CV\% = CV \times 100$.

**Step 5. Interpret.** Compare the CV with that of other variables or with reference thresholds. Low CV = relatively homogeneous; high CV = relatively variable.

**Step 6. Verify the limits.** Check that the mean is not close to zero and that data are positive. If not, do not use the CV.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Height vs weight: comparing different units

Height: mean 170 cm, standard deviation 5 cm. $CV = 5/170 \approx 0.0294$ (2.94 %). Weight: mean 70 kg, standard deviation 8 kg. $CV = 8/70 \approx 0.1143$ (11.43 %). Weight is relatively more variable than height (11.43 % > 2.94 %), even though the absolute standard deviation of weight (8) is larger than that of height (5). Without the CV, you might wrongly conclude that weight is "more variable just because the number 8 is larger than 5".

### Example 2. Two production lines: relative consistency

Line A produces parts with mean 100 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. $CV_A = 2/100 = 0.02$ (2 %). Line B produces parts with mean 50 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. $CV_B = 2/50 = 0.04$ (4 %). Both have the same absolute standard deviation (2 mm), but line B is twice as inconsistent relative to its size. The CV reveals that line A is more stable relative to its target.

### Example 3. Recovering the standard deviation from the CV

An investment has mean return of 8 % and a CV of 25 %. The standard deviation is $s = CV \times \bar{x} = 0.25 \times 8 = 2$ percentage points. That means the return typically deviates about 2 percentage points from the mean of 8 %.

### Example 4. The CV explodes when the mean is close to zero

A variable has mean 0.1 and standard deviation 1. $CV = 1/0.1 = 10$ (1000 %). That huge number does not mean the data are "infinitely variable"; it means the CV is not useful when the mean is close to zero. In that case, use the absolute standard deviation or the IQR.

### Example 5. Prices of two products

Product A: mean price 10 €, standard deviation 1 €. $CV = 1/10 = 0.10$ (10 %). Product B: mean price 100 €, standard deviation 5 €. $CV = 5/100 = 0.05$ (5 %). Product A is relatively more variable (10 % vs 5 %), even though its absolute standard deviation (1 €) is smaller than product B's (5 €).

### Example 6. When NOT to use the CV

Temperatures in degrees Celsius: mean 5 °C, standard deviation 10 °C. $CV = 10/5 = 2$ (200 %). But zero in Celsius does not mean "absence of temperature", and values can be negative. The CV does not make sense here. Use the absolute standard deviation (10 °C) or the IQR.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Comparing standard deviations of variables with different units.** You cannot say weight is "more variable than height" because 8 kg > 5 cm. The units are different. Detection: if someone compares standard deviations of variables with different units without using the CV, they are committing this error. Correction: use the CV to compare across different units.

**Error 2. Comparing standard deviations when means are very different.** A standard deviation of 100 is small if the mean is 10,000 and large if the mean is 150. Detection: if someone judges the standard deviation without looking at the mean, they are ignoring relative scale. Correction: use the CV to put the standard deviation in relation to the mean.

**Error 3. Using the CV with the mean close to zero.** The CV explodes and loses meaning. Detection: if the mean is 0.1 and the standard deviation 1, the CV is 1000 %, which is not interpretable. Correction: use the absolute standard deviation or the IQR when the mean is close to zero.

**Error 4. Using the CV with data that can be negative or zero.** Temperatures in Celsius, profits that can be negative: the CV makes no sense because zero is not a natural point of "absence". Detection: if data cross zero, do not use the CV. Correction: use the absolute standard deviation.

**Error 5. Believing the CV describes a single dataset.** The CV is a relative comparison tool, not an absolute measure. A CV of 20 % is not "good" or "bad" in itself; it only makes sense compared with another CV or with a reference threshold. Detection: if someone says "the CV is 20 %, therefore the data are bad", they are misinterpreting it. Correction: interpret the CV in relation to other CVs or with the context.

**Error 6. Rounding the CV too early.** The CV can be a small number (0.0294) and rounding it to 0.03 loses relative information. Detection: keep enough decimals. Correction: report the CV with at least two decimals or as a percentage with one decimal.

**Error 7. Confusing high CV with "more data".** A high CV indicates more relative variability, not more observations. Detection: if someone says "the CV is high because there are many data", they are confusing concepts. Correction: the CV is independent of sample size.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The CV connects with the standard deviation (it is its relative version) and with the mean (it is in the denominator). It is the bridge between absolute and relative dispersion. In finance, the CV is used to compare the relative risk of different assets: an asset with low mean return and high standard deviation has a high CV (high relative risk). In quality control, the CV compares the consistency of different processes or machines. In biology and social sciences, the CV compares the relative variability of different traits or populations.

The CV also connects with the concept of scale. Being dimensionless, it is invariant to changes of scale: if you multiply all data by a constant (for example, from meters to centimeters), both the mean and the standard deviation are multiplied by that constant, and the CV stays the same. That invariance is what makes comparison across different units possible.

In professional practice, the CV is used as a "relative variability card": before deciding whether a process is stable or an investment is risky, look at the CV in relation to other processes or investments of the same type.

The most useful transfer is the habit of asking: am I comparing apples with oranges? If units or means are different, the CV is the tool; if they are comparable and you want dispersion in real units, use the absolute standard deviation.

**Map of five ideas.** First: the CV is the standard deviation divided by the mean; it is dimensionless and allows comparing dispersion across variables with different units or means.
