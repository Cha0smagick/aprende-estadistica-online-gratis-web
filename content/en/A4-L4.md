## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute the median absolute deviation (MAD) of a dataset: first the median, then the absolute deviations from it, and finally the median of those absolute deviations. Formula: $MAD = \text{median}(|x_i - \tilde{x}|)$. Second, explain in your own words why the MAD is robust to outliers: it uses absolute deviations (not squared) and takes the median of those absolute deviations, so a single extreme value does not shoot it up. Third, compare the MAD with the standard deviation: the MAD is more robust but less used in inference; the standard deviation is more sensitive to extremes but has better algebraic properties. Fourth, understand the factor 1.4826: multiplying the MAD by 1.4826 gives a consistent estimate of the standard deviation when the data follow a normal distribution. Fifth, decide when to use the MAD: when there are outliers or when you want a robust measure of dispersion around the median. Sixth, compute the MAD step by step with a small numerical example verifiable by hand. Seventh, detect the frequent mistake of using only the standard deviation with outliers and not reporting also a robust measure such as the MAD or the IQR. Eighth, connect the MAD with the median and with the IQR as part of the family of robust measures of dispersion.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons: median, range, IQR, variance and standard deviation. You need to know how to compute the median (for odd and even lists), calculate absolute deviations ($|x_i - \tilde{x}|$), and understand what an outlier is. You should have seen the standard deviation to compare it with the MAD. And you should have worked with examples that include an outlier, because that is where the MAD shines.

A thirty-second entry check. Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100. The median is 3. The absolute deviations are |1-3|=2, |2-3|=1, |3-3|=0, |4-3|=1, |100-3|=97, that is, 2, 1, 0, 1, 97. The median of {0, 1, 1, 2, 97} is 1. So the MAD is 1. If you answered 1, you are ready. If you answered something close to 44 (the standard deviation), you forgot that the MAD ignores the magnitude of the 100 beyond its position in the order. If you answered 97 (the range or an individual deviation), you confused concepts. If you hesitated, review the median and absolute deviations before continuing.

A note on scope. The MAD is a robust measure of dispersion, but it does not replace the standard deviation in all situations. In inference (confidence intervals, t-tests), the standard deviation remains the standard because it has better algebraic properties. The MAD is a tool for exploration and robust description, not a universal replacement.

## 2. Why it matters

The standard deviation is the most famous measure of dispersion, but it has a weakness: it squares deviations, so a single extreme value weighs enormously. If you measure the salaries of five employees — 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros — the standard deviation shoots up because of the 200 salary, even though four of the five employees earn between 20 and 26 thousand. That can lead you to conclude that salaries are very variable when, for the typical employee, they are not.

The MAD solves that. Because it uses absolute deviations (not squared) and takes the median of those absolute deviations, a single extreme value barely moves it. In the salary example, the MAD stays small because the central half of the data is concentrated. That makes it invaluable when data have outliers: data entry errors, fraud, customers who spend much more than the rest, or simply distributions with long tails.

It also matters because the MAD is the basis of robust statistical methods. Instead of assuming the data are normal (as the standard deviation does in many inference formulas), the MAD lets you describe dispersion without assuming almost anything about the shape of the distribution. That is key in exploratory data analysis and quality control, where you cannot always trust that the data follow a bell curve.

And there is a practical reason. The factor 1.4826 allows converting the MAD into an estimate of the standard deviation when the data are approximately normal. That gives you the best of both worlds: the robustness of the MAD for computing it and the interpretability of the standard deviation for reporting it.

## 3. Intuition

Picture the data as people in a line ordered by height. The median is the height of the person in the middle. Now, for each person, measure how far they stray from that central height, regardless of whether they are taller or shorter (that is the absolute value of the deviation). That gives you a list of "distances to the center". The MAD is the median of those distances: the typical distance to the center, computed robustly.

Why is it robust? The 100 from the earlier example (the extremely tall person) produces an absolute deviation of 97, but when taking the median of the absolute deviations, that 97 stays at the extreme of the list of deviations and does not affect the central value. The MAD only cares about how many absolute deviations are on each side of its median, not about how large the largest one is.

A useful image. Think of the standard deviation as a scale that punishes distant values heavily (because it squares them). Think of the MAD as a scale that only counts how many values are on each side of the median of the deviations. A very distant value pulls a lot on the first, but almost nothing on the second.

An extreme case to fix the idea. Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100. The median is 3. The absolute deviations are 2, 1, 0, 1, 97. The median of those deviations is 1 (the central value of 0, 1, 1, 2, 97). So the MAD is 1. That tells the truth about the bulk: most of the data are at a typical distance of 1 unit from the median. The standard deviation, in contrast, would be about 44, dominated by the 100. The MAD gives you the honest picture of the central body; the standard deviation gives you the picture of the total including the extreme.

## 4. Definition and notation

Let a sample of $n$ observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ have median $\tilde{x}$. The median absolute deviation (MAD) is defined as:

$$MAD = \text{median}\bigl(|x_1 - \tilde{x}|, |x_2 - \tilde{x}|, \dots, |x_n - \tilde{x}|\bigr)$$

That is, you compute the absolute deviation of each datum from the median, and then take the median of those absolute deviations.

**Steps of the definition:**
1. Compute the median $\tilde{x}$ of the original data.
2. For each datum, compute the absolute deviation $d_i = |x_i - \tilde{x}|$.
3. Sort the absolute deviations $d_1, \dots, d_n$ from smallest to largest.
4. Take the median of those absolute deviations.



MAD vs standard deviation — comparison table:

| Aspect | MAD | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| Reference center | Median | Mean |
| Operation | Absolute deviations, median of those | Squared deviations, average of those |
| Robustness to outliers | High (breakdown point 50 %) | None (breakdown point 0 %) |
| Units | Same as data | Same as data |
| Use in inference | Limited | Standard (variance, standard error, t-tests) |
| Conversion | σ ≈ 1.4826 × MAD (normal data) | — |

**Key properties:**
- The MAD has the same units as the original data (unlike the variance, which has squared units).
- The MAD is robust: its breakdown point is 50 %, meaning you need to corrupt more than half the data to move it arbitrarily. The standard deviation has a breakdown point of 0 %.
- The MAD ignores the magnitude of extreme values beyond their ordered position; it only counts how many are on each side.

**Converting to standard deviation (approximately normal data).** If the data follow a normal distribution, the standard deviation $\sigma$ can be estimated from the MAD as:

$$\sigma \approx 1.4826 \times MAD$$

The factor 1.4826 comes from the fact that, in a normal distribution, the median of the absolute deviations from the median is approximately $0.6745\,\sigma$, and $1/0.6745 \approx 1.4826$.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Compute the median** $\tilde{x}$ of the original data.

**Step 2. Compute the absolute deviations.** For each datum $x_i$, compute $d_i = |x_i - \tilde{x}|$.

**Step 3. Sort the absolute deviations** from smallest to largest.

**Step 4. Take the median of the absolute deviations.** That value is the MAD.

**Step 5. (Optional) Convert to standard deviation.** If the data are approximately normal, multiply the MAD by 1.4826 to obtain an estimate of $\sigma$.

**Step 6. Interpret.** A small MAD means the central half of the data is close to the median; a large MAD means it is dispersed. Compare the MAD with the standard deviation: if the MAD is much smaller than the standard deviation, there are extreme values pulling the latter up.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. MAD of a small list

Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100. The median is 3. The absolute deviations from 3 are: |1-3|=2, |2-3|=1, |3-3|=0, |4-3|=1, |100-3|=97. That is, 2, 1, 0, 1, 97. Sorted: 0, 1, 1, 2, 97. The median of these deviations is the central value: 1. So $MAD = 1$. The standard deviation would be about 44, dominated by the 100. The MAD of 1 describes the central body honestly.

### Example 2. MAD without outliers

Data: 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30. The median is $(18+20)/2 = 19$. The absolute deviations are: |10-19|=9, |12-19|=7, |15-19|=4, |18-19|=1, |20-19|=1, |22-19|=3, |25-19|=6, |30-19|=11. That is, 9, 7, 4, 1, 1, 3, 6, 11. Sorted: 1, 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 11. The median of these deviations (8 values) is $(4+6)/2 = 5$. So $MAD = 5$. The standard deviation would be larger because values 10 and 30 are far from the mean, but the MAD of 5 describes the central body well.

### Example 3. Converting MAD to standard deviation (normal data)

Suppose you have an approximately normal sample with MAD = 2. The estimated standard deviation is $\sigma \approx 1.4826 \times 2 \approx 2.97$. That allows you to report a robustly estimated standard deviation without an outlier distorting it.

### Example 4. Comparing MAD and standard deviation with an outlier

Waiting times (minutes): 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30, 90. Median = 20. Absolute deviations: |10-20|=10, |12-20|=8, |15-20|=5, |18-20|=2, |20-20|=0, |22-20|=2, |25-20|=5, |30-20|=10, |90-20|=70. That is, 10, 8, 5, 2, 0, 2, 5, 10, 70. Sorted: 0, 2, 2, 5, 5, 8, 10, 10, 70. The median of these deviations is the central value (5th): 5. So $MAD = 5$. The standard deviation would be much larger because of the 90. The MAD of 5 says that the central half of waiting times typically deviates 5 minutes from the median of 20.

### Example 5. MAD with repeated values

Data: 5, 5, 5, 5, 100. Median = 5. Absolute deviations: 0, 0, 0, 0, 95. Sorted: 0, 0, 0, 0, 95. The median is 0. So $MAD = 0$. That is correct: four of the five values are identical, so there is no dispersion in the central body. The 100 is an outlier that the MAD ignores.

### Example 6. Common mistake: using only the standard deviation with outliers

A report says "the standard deviation of the salaries is 70 thousand euros" based on 20, 22, 24, 26, 200. That is true for the standard deviation, but misleading for describing the typical employee. The MAD would be small (around 2-3 thousand), reflecting the reality of the majority better. The report should report both or prefer the MAD for the typical case.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Using only the standard deviation with outliers.** This is the most frequent error. The standard deviation shoots up because of a single extreme value. Detection: if a report cites only the standard deviation and there are suspicious outliers, ask for the MAD or the IQR. Correction: report the MAD or IQR alongside the standard deviation, or prefer the MAD when there are outliers.

**Error 2. Believing the MAD is immune to everything.** The MAD is robust, but not magic. If more than 50 % of the data are extreme, the MAD is affected too. Detection: check sample size and histogram. Correction: with small samples or many extremes, do not trust any single measure blindly.

**Error 3. Confusing the MAD with the mean absolute deviation.** The mean absolute deviation is $\frac{1}{n}\sum|x_i - \bar{x}|$ (mean of absolute deviations from the mean). The MAD is the median of absolute deviations from the median. They are different. Detection: if someone uses them as synonyms, they are confusing concepts. Correction: use the MAD when you want robustness with the median as center; use the mean absolute deviation if you need a mean of absolute deviations.

**Error 4. Forgetting to compute the median first.** The MAD requires the median of the original data as center. If you use the mean as center and then take the median of the absolute deviations, that is not the standard MAD. Detection: check that the center is the median, not the mean. Correction: always compute the median first.

**Error 5. Believing the MAD is always smaller than the standard deviation.** Not always. In distributions without outliers and symmetric, the MAD (multiplied by 1.4826) approaches the standard deviation, but the raw MAD is usually smaller than the standard deviation because squared deviations weigh distant values more. Detection: do not assume MAD < standard deviation without computing. Correction: compute both and compare.

**Error 6. Using the 1.4826 factor with non-normal data.** The 1.4826 factor is only valid for approximately normal data. With very skewed data or outliers, the conversion makes no sense. Detection: if someone multiplies the MAD by 1.4826 with clearly non-normal data, they are applying the factor out of context. Correction: use the factor only when you can assume approximate normality.

**Error 7. Reporting the MAD without the context of the median.** Saying "the MAD is 5" without saying the median is 20 is half a report. Detection: a report that gives the MAD without the median. Correction: always report median and MAD together.

**Error 8. Rounding the median or absolute deviations.** Rounding before taking the median of the absolute deviations can bias the result. Detection: keep decimals until the end. Correction: round only when presenting.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The MAD belongs to the family of robust measures of dispersion, along with the IQR and the median. While the IQR measures the width of the central 50 % (from Q1 to Q3), the MAD measures the typical absolute deviation around the median. Both ignore extremes and are complementary: the IQR tells you how much the central body occupies; the MAD tells you how much the data typically deviate from the center.

In inference, the standard deviation remains the standard because it has algebraic properties that the MAD lacks (for example, it appears in the variance, in the standard error and in t-tests). But in exploratory analysis and in situations with outliers, the MAD is preferable. Knowing both lets you choose according to context.

In quality control and finance, the MAD is used to detect unstable processes or assets with atypical behavior without a single extreme value distorting the picture. In data science, the MAD is useful for imputing missing values or scaling features robustly before applying machine learning models.

The most useful transfer is the habit of looking at dispersion with two lenses: one sensitive to extremes (standard deviation) and one robust (MAD or IQR). If both agree, the data are clean and symmetric. If they diverge, there are outliers and you must decide which story you want to tell.
