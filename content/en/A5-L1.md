## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute the quartiles Q1, Q2 (median) and Q3 of a dataset, understanding that they divide the ordered data into four parts of approximately 25 % each. Second, compute any percentile P_k (the value below which k % of the data fall) and explain the difference between percentile and quartile (quartiles are particular percentiles: Q1 = P_25, Q2 = P_50, Q3 = P_75). Third, interpret a percentile in context: if your score is at the 80th percentile, it means 80 % of people scored the same or less, not that you scored 80 %. Fourth, compute the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 - Q1) from the quartiles and use it to describe the dispersion of the central body. Fifth, apply Tukey's rule to detect outliers (below Q1 - 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR). Sixth, compute quartiles and percentiles from a cumulative frequency table without listing every datum. Seventh, detect the frequent mistake of confusing "being at the 90th percentile" with "scoring 90 %". Eighth, connect quartiles and percentiles with boxplots and with the median you saw before.

The prerequisites are the previous central tendency and dispersion lessons: median, range and IQR. You need to sort data from smallest to largest, compute the median and understand cumulative frequency. And you should have worked with examples that include percentiles (exams, child growth, income) so the idea is not abstract.

A thirty-second entry check. Data: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. What is Q1 (25th percentile)? If you answered 10 (or a value between 5 and 15 depending on convention), you are entering the topic. What is the 50th percentile? It is the median, 15. What does it mean that a student is at the 90th percentile of a test? If you answered "that 90 % of students scored the same or less", you are ready. If you answered "that he scored 90 %", you have confused percentile with score; this lesson exists to separate both ideas. If you hesitated between both, review the median before continuing.

A note on scope. Percentiles and quartiles describe the relative position of a datum within a set, not its absolute value. A 90th percentile on an easy test can correspond to a low score in points, and a 10th percentile on a hard test can correspond to a high score. Always interpret the percentile together with the context.

## 2. Why it matters

Percentiles are one of the most used tools in daily life, though they are rarely called by their name. Pediatricians use growth percentiles to say whether a baby weighs more or less than 90 % of babies of the same age. Universities use admission score percentiles to compare candidates from different schools. Parents use height percentiles to know if their child is growing as expected. Income reports use percentiles to say how much the poorest 10 % or the richest 10 % earn.

It also matters because the percentile tells you where you stand relative to others, not how much you are worth in absolute terms. A score of 70 on an exam can be the 95th percentile if the exam was hard, or the 10th percentile if the exam was easy. Without the percentile, the number 70 by itself tells you nothing about your relative position.

It also matters because quartiles and the IQR are the basis of boxplots, which summarize at a glance the shape, dispersion and extremes of a dataset. Without understanding quartiles and percentiles, you don't read boxplots well.

And there is a practical reason. In public policy and business, percentiles are used to define thresholds: the income that separates the poorest 20 %, the score that separates the best 10 % for a scholarship, the delivery time that separates the slowest 5 %. Knowing how to compute and interpret percentiles is a concrete skill used in real decisions.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine putting 100 people in a line ordered by height, from shortest to tallest. The 50th percentile is the height of the person in position 50: the median. The 25th percentile is the height of the person in position 25: 25 % of people are shorter or equal, and 75 % are taller or equal. The 90th percentile is the height of the person in position 90: only 10 % are taller.

A useful image. Think of percentiles as marks on a ruler that measure relative position, not absolute length. The percentile does not tell you "you measure 1.70 meters"; it tells you "you measure more than 60 % of people". That is different information and, in many contexts, more useful.

An extreme case. If all data are equal (everyone measures 1.70), all percentiles are 1.70. There is no dispersion, and the percentile tells you nothing useful about variation, only about position (which is the same for everyone). If there is an extreme value (a person of 2.50 meters), the low and middle percentiles barely move, because the extreme only affects the 100th percentile. That is a form of robustness: percentiles are not fooled as much by a single extreme value as the mean is.

Finally, the difference between quartile and percentile. Quartiles are simply percentiles with special names: Q1 is the 25th percentile, Q2 is the 50th percentile (the median) and Q3 is the 75th percentile. They are not distinct concepts; they are the same concept with special names for the most used points.

## 4. Definition and notation

Let a sample of $n$ observations be ordered from smallest to largest: $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$.

**Percentile P_k.** The percentile $P_k$ (for $0 \le k \le 100$) is the value below which approximately $k$ % of the data fall. Several conventions exist for computing it exactly when the result falls between two data; the most common is to interpolate between neighboring values.

**Quartiles.** Quartiles are particular percentiles:
- Q1 = P_25: the value that leaves 25 % of the data below.
- Q2 = P_50: the median. Leaves 50 % below and 50 % above.
- Q3 = P_75: the value that leaves 75 % of the data below.

**Interquartile range (IQR).** $IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = P_{75} - P_{25}$. It measures the width of the central 50 % of the data.

**Tukey's rule for outliers.** A value is suspect of being an outlier if it is below $Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR$.

**Interpretation of a percentile.** If a datum is at percentile $k$, it means that approximately $k$ % of the data are less than or equal to it, and $(100-k)$ % are greater than or equal to it. It does not mean the datum "is worth k %".

**Limiting cases.** If all data are equal, all percentiles are that same value. If $n$ is small, percentiles depend on the interpolation convention and can be unstable. Percentiles require orderable data (ordinal or quantitative); they make no sense for unordered categorical data (colors, brands).

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Sort the data.** Percentiles and quartiles require data ordered from smallest to largest.

**Step 2. Decide which percentile you want.** The 25th (Q1), the 50th (median), the 75th (Q3) or another (P_90, P_10, etc.)?

**Step 3. Compute the position.** For percentile $P_k$, the approximate position is:

$$pos = \frac{k}{100} \times n$$

If the position is an integer, take that value; if not, interpolate between the two neighboring values.

**Step 4. For quartiles.** Q1 is the median of the lower half; Q2 is the median of all data; Q3 is the median of the upper half.

**Step 5. Compute the IQR.** Subtract Q1 from Q3: $IQR = Q_3 - Q_1$.

**Step 6. Apply Tukey's rule.** Lower limit: $Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR$; upper limit: $Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR$. Any datum outside is a candidate outlier.

**Step 7. Interpret.** Say in which percentile a concrete datum falls and what that means in the context of the problem.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Quartiles of a small list

Data: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Already sorted. Median Q2 = 15 (central value of 5). Lower half: {5, 10}, Q1 = $(5+10)/2 = 7.5$. Upper half: {20, 25}, Q3 = $(20+25)/2 = 22.5$. IQR: $22.5 - 7.5 = 15$. The quartiles are 7.5, 15 and 22.5.

### Example 2. Interpreting a percentile in a test

A test of 20 students. A student obtains a score that places them at the 80th percentile. That means 80 % of students obtained that score or less, and 20 % obtained more. It does not mean the student scored 80 % correct. If the student scored 72 out of 100 and is at the 80th percentile, their absolute score is 72, but their relative position is high.

### Example 3. Percentiles in child growth

A 6-month-old baby weighs 8 kg. According to growth charts, that weight is at the 60th percentile for babies of that age. That means 60 % of 6-month-old babies weigh 8 kg or less, and 40 % weigh more. The baby is within the normal range (neither extremely low nor extremely high).

### Example 4. Quartiles from a frequency table

A survey of 40 households about number of cars:

| Cars | Households | Cumulative frequency |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 5 | 5 |
| 1 | 12 | 17 |
| 2 | 15 | 32 |
| 3 | 6 | 38 |
| 4 | 2 | 40 |

$n = 40$. Median Q2: position 20 (50 % of 40). Cumulative frequency reaches 17 with 1 car and 32 with 2 cars, so position 20 falls in 2 cars: Q2 = 2. Q1: position 10 (25 % of 40). Cumulative reaches 17 with 1 car, so position 10 falls in 1 car: Q1 = 1. Q3: position 30 (75 % of 40). Cumulative reaches 32 with 2 cars, so position 30 falls in 2 cars: Q3 = 2. IQR: $2 - 1 = 1$ car. The central 50 % of households have between 1 and 2 cars.

### Example 5. Tukey's rule with the quartiles from example 4

Q1 = 1, Q3 = 2, IQR = 1. Lower limit: $1 - 1.5\times1 = -0.5$. Upper limit: $2 + 1.5\times1 = 3.5$. Any household with fewer than -0.5 or more than 3.5 cars is an outlier according to Tukey. Since the maximum is 4 cars, the household with 4 cars is an outlier according to Tukey (although only one).

### Example 6. 90th percentile vs score 90

Two students. Ana scores 72 out of 100 and is at the 80th percentile. Bruno scores 85 out of 100 and is at the 90th percentile. Bruno has a higher absolute score (85 > 72) and a higher relative position (90th percentile > 80th). But if in another test Bruno scores 85 and is at the 50th percentile (because the exam was easy and almost everyone scored 90), his absolute score is the same but his relative position is median. The percentile tells you the position; the score tells you the absolute value. Do not confuse them.

### Example 7. Quartiles and outliers with an extreme value

Data: 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 100. Median Q2 = 16. Lower half: {10, 12, 14}, Q1 = 12. Upper half: {18, 20, 100}, Q3 = 20. IQR: $20 - 12 = 8$. Tukey's upper limit: $20 + 1.5\times8 = 20 + 12 = 32$. The 100 exceeds 32: it is an outlier according to Tukey. The quartiles (12, 16, 20) barely move because of the 100, while the mean and standard deviation would shoot up.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Confusing percentile with score.** "Being at the 90th percentile" does not mean "scoring 90 %". It means 90 % of the data are equal to or less than it. Detection: if someone says "I am at the 90th percentile" to refer to a score of 90 out of 100, they are confusing concepts. Correction: remember that the percentile is a relative position, not an absolute value.

**Error 2. Believing the 100th percentile is the maximum.** In some conventions, the 100th percentile is the maximum, but in others the maximum possible percentile is less than 100 (for example, if you use interpolation convention, the largest value can fall at the 99.x percentile). Detection: do not assume the 100th percentile is always the maximum without checking the convention. Correction: interpret the highest percentile available in your context.

**Error 3. Using percentiles with unordered categorical data.** It makes no sense to compute the 50th percentile of favorite colors (red, blue, green) because there is no natural order. Detection: ask whether you can say one value is "greater" than another. Correction: for unordered categorical data, use the mode.

**Error 4. Forgetting to sort before computing.** Percentiles require sorted data. Detection: if you compute the percentile over unsorted data, the result is random. Correction: sorting is step 1.

**Error 5. Confusing Q1 with 25 % of the sum.** Q1 is the value that leaves 25 % of the data below, not 25 % of the sum of the values nor 25 % of the maximum. Detection: if someone says "Q1 is 25 % of the maximum value", they are confusing concepts. Correction: remember that quartiles are ordered positions, not percentages of magnitude.

**Error 6. Believing a high percentile is always "good".** It depends on context. A 90th percentile in emergency waiting time is bad (you waited more than 90 % of patients). A 90th percentile in exam score is good. Detection: interpret the percentile in the context of the problem; do not assume high = good. Correction: always ask "is it better to be above or below in this variable?".

**Error 7. Rounding quartiles.** Q1 or Q3 can be intermediate values (median of two values). Rounding them biases the IQR. Detection: if you report Q1 = 7 instead of 7.5 because "it looks cleaner", you are introducing error. Correction: keep decimals and round only when presenting.

**Error 8. Believing all percentiles exist for any dataset.** If all data are equal, all percentiles are that value and there is no dispersion information. If $n$ is very small, percentiles are unstable. Detection: check sample size and variability before trusting a percentile as a definitive conclusion.

## 8. Connections and transfer

Quartiles and percentiles connect directly with the IQR and with boxplots, which show the minimum, Q1, the median, Q3 and the maximum, with outliers marked as points outside Tukey's fences. Without understanding quartiles and percentiles, you don't read boxplots well.

They also connect with the median (Q2 is the 50th percentile) and with the median absolute deviation (MAD, which uses the median as center). And they connect with inference: percentiles are used to build non-parametric confidence intervals and to define decision thresholds in public policy and business.

In professional practice, percentiles are the standard for reporting relative position: admission scores, child growth, income, delivery times. When you read a report that cites only a mean without percentiles, be suspicious: the mean can hide where the majority of the data really lie.

The most useful transfer is the habit of asking: where do I stand relative to others? Before focusing on an absolute value, ask what percentile it occupies. That question gives you more information about your relative position than the absolute value by itself.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Data: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. What are Q1, Q2 and Q3? — **Solution:** Q1 = 7.5, Q2 = 15, Q3 = 22.5.
2. A student is at the 80th percentile of a test. What does it mean? — **Solution:** That 80 % of students obtained that score or less, and 20 % obtained more. It does not mean they scored 80 %.
3. A frequency table of 40 households: 0 cars (5), 1 car (12), 2 cars (15), 3 cars (6), 4 cars (2). What are the median and the IQR? — **Solution:** Median Q2 = 2 (position 20 falls in 2 cars). Q1 = 1 (position 10 falls in 1 car). Q3 = 2 (position 30 falls in 2 cars). IQR = 2 - 1 = 1 car.
4. Q1 = 1, Q3 = 2, IQR = 1. What is Tukey's upper limit? — **Solution:** $2 + 1.5\times1 = 3.5$. Any value above 3.5 is an outlier.
5. Two students: Ana scores 72 and is at the 80th percentile; Bruno scores 85 and is at the 50th percentile on another test. Who has the better relative position? — **Solution:** Ana (80th percentile > 50th percentile), although Bruno has a higher absolute score. The percentile measures relative position, not absolute value.
6. Explain in two sentences why a 90th percentile in waiting time is bad but a 90th percentile in exam score is good. — **Solution:** Because the percentile only tells you your relative position, not whether that position is desirable. In waiting time, being above 90 % of patients means you waited more than almost everyone (bad); in exam score, being above 90 % of students means you scored more than almost everyone (good).
**Map of five ideas.** First: percentiles tell you your relative position within an ordered set; quartiles are particular percentiles (P_25, P_50, P_75). Second: a percentile $k$ means $k$ % of the data are equal to or less than it; it does not mean the datum "is worth $k$ %". Third: the IQR (Q3 - Q1) measures the width of the central 50 % and is robust to outliers. Fourth: Tukey's rule ($Q_1 - 1.5\times IQR$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\times IQR$) detects outliers. Fifth: always interpret the percentile in context; high is not always good.
