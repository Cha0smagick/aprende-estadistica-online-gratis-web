## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute the z-score of a datum: $z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}$ (sample) or $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ (population), and interpret what it means. Second, understand that the z-score standardizes: it converts any variable to a common scale with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, which allows comparing variables with different units. Third, interpret the sign and magnitude of a z: $z=0$ is at the mean, $z>0$ above, $z<0$ below, and $|z|>2$ usually indicates an unusual value in normal distributions. Fourth, recover the original value from a z: $x = \bar{x} + z\cdot s$ (or $x = \mu + z\cdot\sigma$). Fifth, decide when to use the z-score to compare observations from different distributions (for example, exam scores with different scales). Sixth, connect the z-score with the empirical rule (68-95-99.7 %) that you will see in the next lesson. Seventh, detect the frequent mistake of comparing raw values of different variables without standardizing. Eighth, recognize that the z-score does not eliminate outliers or skewness: it only recenters and rescales.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons on mean and standard deviation. You need to know how to compute the mean and the standard deviation (population and sample). You need to understand what a deviation ($x - \bar{x}$) is and why dividing it by the standard deviation puts it on a scale of "standard deviations". And you should have seen examples of comparing variables (exam scores, prices, heights) so that the usefulness of the z-score is clear.

A thirty-second entry check. A student scores 85 on an exam with mean 70 and standard deviation 10. What is their z-score? If you answered $(85-70)/10 = 1.5$, you are ready. Another: if a z-score is -2, is the datum above or below the mean? If you answered "below, by two standard deviations", you are ready. If you answered anything else, review the standard deviation before continuing.

A note on scope.

In finance, the z-score appears in the Sharpe ratio, which measures excess return per unit of risk (standard deviation of returns). A high Sharpe ratio means you are obtaining more return for each unit of volatility you assume. That is, in essence, a z-score applied to returns: how many standard deviations of excess return you obtain per unit of risk. In quality control, z-scores are used in control charts to detect when a process goes out of its usual limits. If a production measure has z > 3, the process probably needs review. These applications show that the z-score is not just a classroom exercise: it is a concrete decision-making tool.

 The z-score is a standardization tool, not a correction tool. It does not eliminate outliers, does not fix skewed data, and does not turn any distribution into a bell curve. It only recenters and rescales. Using it as if it were a cure for all distribution problems is a common mistake.

## 2. Why it matters

The z-score matters because it solves a very common problem: how do you compare two values measured in different units or on different scales? If a student scores 80 in math (mean 70, standard deviation 10) and 75 in English (mean 65, standard deviation 5), in which subject did they do better relative to their classmates? At first glance, 80 > 75, but in English their 75 is farther from the mean relative to the dispersion of that class. The z-score answers that: math $z = (80-70)/10 = 1.0$; English $z = (75-65)/5 = 2.0$. In English they did better relative to the group.

It also matters because the z-score is the foundation of many inferential techniques. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and significance tests work with z-scores (or with t-scores, their cousin for small samples). Without understanding the z-score, you don't understand why we say "this result is significant at two standard deviations".

It also matters because the z-score tells you how "unusual" a datum is. In an approximately normal distribution, 68 % of the data lie between $z = -1$ and $z = 1$, 95 % between $z = -2$ and $z = 2$, and 99.7 % between $z = -3$ and $z = 3$. A z-score of 3 or more is a very unusual value (only 0.3 % of the data lie beyond it). That helps you detect outliers without relying solely on the IQR.

And there is a practical reason. In personnel selection, university admissions or quality control, z-scores are used to create composite scores: if a candidate has z-scores on several tests, you can average them to obtain a general standardized score. Without standardizing, you would be adding apples and oranges.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine you have two math classes. In class A, the exam mean is 70 and the standard deviation is 10. In class B, the mean is 60 and the standard deviation is 20. A student scores 80 in class A and another scores 80 in class B. Who did better? In class A, 80 is one standard deviation above the mean ($z = 1.0$). In class B, 80 is one standard deviation above the mean too ($z = (80-60)/20 = 1.0$). In this case, they did equally well relative to their groups.

But now imagine the student in class B scores 90. Then $z = (90-60)/20 = 1.5$, which is better than the $z = 1.0$ of class A, even though 90 and 80 are different raw values. The z-score tells you that, relative to their group, the student in class B with 90 had a more outstanding performance than the student in class A with 80.

The central intuition is this: the z-score answers the question "how far is this datum from the mean, measured in standard deviations?". It is like changing rulers: instead of measuring in meters or pounds, you measure in "units of standard deviation". That lets you compare things that were previously incomparable.

An extreme case. A datum with $z = 0$ is exactly the mean. A datum with $z = -3$ is three standard deviations below the mean: very unusual, only 0.15 % of the data in a normal distribution lie below that. A datum with $z = +2.5$ is two and a half standard deviations above the mean: also very unusual, only around 0.6 % lie above it.

## 4. Definition and notation

**Z-score of a sample.** For a datum $x$ from a sample with mean $\bar{x}$ and standard deviation $s$, the z-score is

$$z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}$$

**Z-score of a population.** For a datum $x$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the z-score is

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

**Interpretation of sign and magnitude:**
- $z = 0$: the datum is exactly equal to the mean.
- $z > 0$: the datum is above the mean; the z value tells you how many standard deviations above.
- $z < 0$: the datum is below the mean; the z value tells you how many standard deviations below.
- $|z| > 2$: in normal distributions, usually indicates an unusual value (approximately 5 % of the data lie outside this range).
- $|z| > 3$: very unusual value (approximately 0.3 % of the data).

**Recovering the original value.** If you know the z, the mean and the standard deviation, you can recover the original value:

$$x = \bar{x} + z \cdot s \quad \text{(sample)} \qquad x = \mu + z \cdot \sigma \quad \text{(population)}$$

**

Quick reference table — typical z-scores:

| z-score | Position relative to the mean | Approximate frequency (normal distribution) |
|---:|:---|:---:|
| 0 | At the mean | 50 % below |
| 1 | 1 standard deviation above | ~84 % below |
| 2 | 2 standard deviations above | ~97.5 % below |
| 3 | 3 standard deviations above | ~99.9 % below |
| -1 | 1 standard deviation below | ~16 % below |
| -2 | 2 standard deviations below | ~2.5 % below |

Properties of the z-score:**
- The z-score is dimensionless: it has no units (not "euros" nor "cm", it is a pure number).
- If you transform all data of a sample into z-scores, the new mean is 0 and the new standard deviation is 1.
- The z-score does not change the shape of the distribution: if the original data are skewed, the z-scores will be skewed too. It only recenters and rescales.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Compute the mean and the standard deviation.** You need $\bar{x}$ and $s$ (sample) or $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (population).

**Step 2. Subtract the mean from the datum.** Compute $x - \bar{x}$ or $x - \mu$. That is the deviation of the datum from the center.

**Step 3. Divide by the standard deviation.** Compute $z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}$ or $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$. That is the number of standard deviations separating the datum from the mean.

**Step 4. Interpret.** Sign: above or below the mean. Magnitude: how many standard deviations. $|z| > 2$: unusual value in normal distributions.

**Step 5. (Optional) Recover the original value.** $x = \bar{x} + z\cdot s$ or $x = \mu + z\cdot\sigma$.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Exam score

A student scores 85 on an exam with mean 70 and standard deviation 10. The z-score is

$$z = \frac{85 - 70}{10} = \frac{15}{10} = 1.5$$

It is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean. That is a good relative performance: approximately 93 % of students scored below them in a normal distribution.

### Example 2. Score below the mean

Another student scores 55 on the same exam (mean 70, standard deviation 10).

$$z = \frac{55 - 70}{10} = \frac{-15}{10} = -1.5$$

It is 1.5 standard deviations below the mean. Approximately 7 % of students scored below them in a normal distribution.

### Example 3. Comparing two exams with different scales

Math: you score 80, mean 70, standard deviation 10. English: you score 75, mean 65, standard deviation 5.

- Math: $z = (80-70)/10 = 1.0$.
- English: $z = (75-65)/5 = 2.0$.

Although 80 > 75 in absolute value, relative to each group you did better in English ($z = 2.0$ versus $z = 1.0$). Without the z-score, you would conclude wrongly.

### Example 4. Recovering the original value

A z-score of 1.5, with mean 60 and standard deviation 8. What is the original value?

$$x = 60 + 1.5 \times 8 = 60 + 12 = 72$$

The original value is 72. Check: $(72-60)/8 = 12/8 = 1.5$. Correct.

### Example 5. Detecting an unusual value

Product prices are normally distributed with mean 100 € and standard deviation 15 €. A price of 155 € has

$$z = \frac{155 - 100}{15} = \frac{55}{15} \approx 3.67$$

A z of 3.67 is very unusual: only around 0.01 % of prices lie above that in a normal distribution. It is very likely a data entry error or an exceptional case.

### Example 6. The z-score does not eliminate outliers

Data: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 100. The mean is 18.125 and the standard deviation is approximately 32.9. The z-score of the 100 is $(100-18.125)/32.9 \approx 2.49$. It is unusual, but not extreme according to the $|z|>2$ rule (it is right at the limit). However, the 100 clearly distorts the mean and the standard deviation. The z-score warns you it is unusual, but does not tell you what to do with it: that depends on context.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Comparing raw values of different variables.** "I scored 80 in math and 75 in English, so I did better in math". False: it depends on the mean and standard deviation of each exam. The z-score is the correct tool for comparing. Detection: if someone compares raw values of different units or scales without standardizing, they are committing this error. Correction: compute the z-scores and compare those.

**Error 2. Believing the z-score eliminates outliers.** The z-score warns you that a value is unusual, but it does not remove or correct it. Also, because the z-score uses the mean and standard deviation, an extreme outlier can inflate the standard deviation and make its own z-score smaller than it appears. Detection: if someone uses the z-score to "clean" data without examining the context, they are committing this error. Correction: use the z-score as a diagnosis, not a cure.

**Error 3. Assuming any distribution with z-scores is normal.** The z-score only recenters and rescales; it does not change the shape. If the original data are skewed, the z-scores will be skewed too. Detection: if someone says "since I computed z-scores, my data are normal", they are overstating. Correction: use histograms or normality tests to verify the shape.

**Error 4. Confusing z-score with percentage.** A z of 1.0 does not mean 100 % or 1 %; it means one standard deviation above the mean. In a normal distribution, it corresponds approximately to the 84th percentile. Detection: if someone says "my z is 1.5, so I am at 150 %", they are confusing concepts. Correction: remember that z measures standard deviations, not percentages.

**Error 5. Using the population standard deviation with a sample (or vice versa).** If you have a sample, use $s$ in the denominator; if you have the population, use $\sigma$. Mixing them biases the result. Detection: ask whether you have the whole population or only a sample. Correction: sample → $s$; population → $\sigma$.

**Error 6. Rounding the z too early.** A z of 1.54 rounded to 1.5 can change the interpretation in borderline cases (for example, if you are close to $z = 2$ as an "unusual" threshold). Detection: keep decimals in intermediate calculations. Correction: round only when presenting.

**Error 7. Believing $|z| > 2$ always means "outlier".** In normal distributions, $|z| > 2$ is unusual (approximately 5 % of the data). But in non-normal distributions, that threshold does not have the same meaning. Detection: if someone applies $|z| > 2$ as an absolute rule without looking at the shape of the distribution, they are overgeneralizing. Correction: use $|z| > 2$ as a guide, not a sentence, and complement it with the IQR or the MAD.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The z-score connects directly with the empirical rule (68-95-99.7 %) that you will see in the next lesson: that rule is basically a table of z-scores for normal distributions. It also connects with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, where an observed z-score is compared with critical values (for example, $z = 1.96$ for a 5 % significance level).

In multivariate statistics, the z-score is the first step to compute Pearson's correlation: correlation is basically the average of the product of the z-scores of two variables. Without understanding the z-score, you don't understand correlation.

In machine learning, standardization (subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation) is a standard preprocessing step before many algorithms (regression, k-means, neural networks). The z-score is exactly that standardization.

In professional practice, z-scores are used to create composite scores (for example, in university admissions or personnel selection), to detect unusual values in quality control, and to compare relative performance in finance (Sharpe ratio, for example).

The most useful transfer is the habit of asking: how far is this value from the mean, in units of standard deviation? That question, more than the raw value, tells you whether a datum is typical or unusual.

2. A datum $x = 30$, mean $\mu = 40$, standard deviation $\sigma = 5$. What is the z-score? — **Solution:** $z = (30-40)/5 = -10/5 = -2$. It is two standard deviations below the mean.

5. Math: you score 80, mean 70, standard deviation 10. English: you score 75, mean 65, standard deviation 5. In which subject did you do better relative to the group? — **Solution:** Math: $z = 1.0$. English: $z = 2.0$. You did better in English relative to the group, although 80 > 75 in absolute value.
6. Explain in two sentences why the z-score does not eliminate outliers nor make a distribution normal. — **Solution:** Because the z-score only recenters (subtracts the mean) and rescales (divides by the standard deviation); it does not change the shape. **Map of five ideas.** First: the z-score measures how many standard deviations separate a datum from the mean; it is the standardization tool. Second: $z = 0$ is the mean, $z > 0$ above, $z < 0$ below; $|z| > 2$ usually indicates an unusual value in normal distributions. Third: it allows comparing variables with different units or scales (for example, different exam scores). Fourth: you recover the original value with $x = \bar{x} + z\cdot s$. Fifth: the z-score does not eliminate outliers nor normalize the distribution; it only recenters and rescales.
