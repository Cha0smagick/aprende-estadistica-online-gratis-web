## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, state the empirical rule (68-95-99.7 %) for approximately normal distributions: 68 % of the data fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 % within two, and 99.7 % within three. Second, compute intervals using the rule: $\bar{x} \pm s$, $\bar{x} \pm 2s$, $\bar{x} \pm 3s$ (or with $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for population). Third, interpret a z-score in terms of the empirical rule: a z of 1, 2 or 3 corresponds to approximately the 84th, 97.5th and 99.85th percentiles respectively. Fourth, decide when to apply the empirical rule and when not to: only for approximately normal and symmetric distributions, not for very skewed data or heavy tails. Fifth, detect the frequent mistake of applying the empirical rule to any dataset without checking the shape. Sixth, connect the empirical rule with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests that you will see in inference. Seventh, use the empirical rule to detect unusual values: a z greater than 2 or less than -2 is unusual in a normal distribution. Eighth, recognize the limits of the rule: it is an approximation, not an exact law, and it does not work for data with high kurtosis or strong skewness.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons: mean, standard deviation, z-score, skewness and kurtosis. You need to know how to compute the mean and the standard deviation, interpret a z-score and understand what an approximately normal distribution (bell curve) is. And you should have seen examples of data that follow a bell curve (heights, standardized exam scores, measurement errors) so that the empirical rule makes sense.

A thirty-second entry check. A normal distribution has mean 100 and standard deviation 15. What percentage of the data falls between 85 and 115 (one standard deviation)? If you answered approximately 68 %, you are ready. And between 70 and 130 (two standard deviations)? If you answered approximately 95 %, you are ready. If you answered that it cannot be known without more data, review the standard deviation and the z-score before continuing.

A note on scope. The empirical rule is an approximation for approximately normal distributions. It is not an exact law that applies to all data. Applying it to highly skewed data or heavy tails leads to wrong conclusions. Use it as a quick guide, not as a definitive sentence.

## 2. Why it matters

The empirical rule is one of the most useful tools of descriptive statistics because it lets you say, in seconds, how "typical" or "unusual" a value is within an approximately normal distribution. Without complex tables or heavy calculations, you can estimate what proportion of the data falls within a certain range around the mean.

It also matters because it is the intuitive foundation of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. When you say "the confidence interval is mean ± 2 standard errors", you are applying a version of the empirical rule: approximately 95 % of sample means fall within that range if the distribution is normal. Understanding the empirical rule gives you the intuition behind those intervals before you get to the formal formulas of inference.

It also matters in quality control and anomaly detection. If a manufacturing process produces parts with mean 10 mm and standard deviation 0.1 mm, a part of 10.3 mm has z = 3, which according to the empirical rule occurs in only 0.3 % of cases. That warns you that something may be wrong in the process. Without the empirical rule, you would not have a quick reference to judge whether a value is "normal" or "unusual".

And there is a practical reason: in exploratory data analysis, the empirical rule gives you a quick first impression of whether your data behave like a bell curve. If approximately 68 % of your data do not fall within one standard deviation of the mean, something is not normal: it may be skewness, heavy tails or mixed data. That tells you that you need different methods before applying parametric techniques.

## 3. Intuition

Picture a bell curve: symmetric, with a peak at the center (the mean) and tails that fade away toward both sides. The empirical rule tells you how much "mass" of data falls at certain distances from the center, measured in standard deviations.

Within one standard deviation ($\bar{x} \pm s$): approximately 68 % of the data. That means two of every three observations are close to the mean, within one standard deviation of distance.

Within two standard deviations ($\bar{x} \pm 2s$): approximately 95 % of the data. Almost all observations are within that range; only one out of twenty falls outside.

Within three standard deviations ($\bar{x} \pm 3s$): approximately 99.7 % of the data. Almost all observations are within that range; only three out of a thousand fall outside. A value outside that range is very unusual.

A useful image. Think of the standard deviation as a "typical distance unit". The empirical rule tells you how many data points are at 1, 2 and 3 typical units from the center. It is like having a quick map of the territory: most people live close to the center (68 %); almost everyone lives within two units (95 %); almost nobody lives more than three units away (99.7 %).

An extreme case. If you have data with heavy tails (positive kurtosis), the empirical rule fails: more than 5 % of the data will fall outside two standard deviations, not the 5 % that the normal predicts. If you have skewed data, the rule also fails because the 68 % is not distributed symmetrically around the mean. The empirical rule assumes symmetry and normal tails; without that, it is a broken compass.

## 4. Definition and notation

For an approximately normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ (population) or $\bar{x}$ and $s$ (sample), the empirical rule states:

- Approximately 68 % of the data fall within the interval $[\mu - \sigma, \mu + \sigma]$ (or $[\bar{x} - s, \bar{x} + s]$ for sample).
- Approximately 95 % of the data fall within the interval $[\mu - 2\sigma, \mu + 2\sigma]$ (or $[\bar{x} - 2s, \bar{x} + 2s]$).
- Approximately 99.7 % of the data fall within the interval $[\mu - 3\sigma, \mu + 3\sigma]$ (or $[\bar{x} - 3s, \bar{x} + 3s]$).

**Connection with the z-score.** A z-score of 1 corresponds to approximately the 84th percentile (84 % of the data are below). A z of 2 corresponds to approximately the 97.5th percentile. A z of 3 corresponds to approximately the 99.85th percentile. That derives directly from the empirical rule: if 95 % lie between $z = -2$ and $z = 2$, then 2.5 % lie above $z = 2$, so the percentile of $z = 2$ is approximately 97.5.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Verify that the data are approximately normal.** Look at a histogram or the skewness and kurtosis. If they are not approximately normal, do not apply the empirical rule.

**Step 2. Compute the mean and the standard deviation.** $\bar{x}$ and $s$ (sample) or $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (population).

**Step 3. Build the intervals.** Compute $\bar{x} \pm s$, $\bar{x} \pm 2s$ and $\bar{x} \pm 3s$ (or with $\mu$ and $\sigma$).

**Step 4. Interpret.** Approximately 68 % of the data fall in the first interval, 95 % in the second and 99.7 % in the third. A value outside the third interval is very unusual.

**Step 5. Relate to z-scores.** A z of 1, 2 or 3 corresponds respectively to the edges of those intervals. A datum with $z > 2$ or $z < -2$ is unusual in a normal distribution.



### Quick interpretation of z-scores

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations separate a datum from the mean. In an approximately normal distribution:
- $z = 1$: approximately the 84th percentile (84 % of the data are below).
- $z = 2$: approximately the 97.5th percentile.
- $z = 3$: approximately the 99.85th percentile.

So a z of 2 or more (or of -2 or less) signals an unusual value in a normal distribution.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Adult heights

Adult men's heights have mean 175 cm and standard deviation 7 cm (approximately normal). Then:
- Approximately 68 % of men measure between 168 cm and 182 cm ($175 \pm 7$).
- Approximately 95 % measure between 161 cm and 189 cm ($175 \pm 14$).
- Approximately 99.7 % measure between 154 cm and 196 cm ($175 \pm 21$).
A man who measures 195 cm has $z = (195-175)/7 \approx 2.86$, very unusual: less than 0.3 % of men measure that or more.

### Example 2. Standardized exam scores

An exam has mean 500 and standard deviation 100 (approximately normal). Then:
- Approximately 68 % of students score between 400 and 600.
- Approximately 95 % score between 300 and 700.
- Approximately 99.7 % score between 200 and 800.
A student who scores 650 has $z = (650-500)/100 = 1.5$. That places them approximately between the 84th and 97.5th percentiles (more precisely, around the 93rd percentile in an exact normal table).

### Example 3. Detecting an unusual value in quality control

A machine produces screws with mean length 10 mm and standard deviation 0.1 mm (approximately normal). A screw measures 10.35 mm. Its z-score is $(10.35-10)/0.1 = 3.5$. That is more than three standard deviations from the mean: it occurs in less than 0.3 % of cases. It is very likely a process problem or a measurement error.

### Example 4. Common mistake: applying the rule to skewed data

A company's salaries have mean 58.4 thousand euros and standard deviation 74.6 thousand euros, but they are highly skewed (right tail toward high salaries). If you apply the empirical rule, you would say that 95 % of salaries fall between $58.4 - 2\times74.6 = -90.8$ and $58.4 + 2\times74.6 = 207.6$ thousand euros. That is absurd: no salary is negative, and the range does not reflect the reality of the majority. The empirical rule does not apply here because the data are not approximately normal.

### Example 5. Relating z-score to percentile

A student has z = 2 on an exam (mean 70, standard deviation 10). According to the empirical rule, 95 % of students lie between $z = -2$ and $z = 2$, so 2.5 % lie above $z = 2$. The student is approximately at the 97.5th percentile (in an exact normal table, the percentile of z = 2 is 97.72). That means they scored better than approximately 97.5 % of their classmates.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Applying the empirical rule to data that are not approximately normal.** This is the most frequent error. The rule only works for symmetric distributions with tails like the normal. Detection: if someone applies the 68-95-99.7 % rule to salaries, house prices or financial returns without checking the shape, they are committing this error. Correction: verify first the symmetry and kurtosis; if they are not approximately normal, use percentiles or the median and IQR instead.

**Error 2. Believing the empirical rule is exact.** It is an approximation ("empirical rule", not exact law). In a real normal distribution, the percentages are 68.27 %, 95.45 % and 99.73 %, not exactly 68, 95 and 99.7. Detection: if someone treats the numbers as exact, they are oversimplifying. Correction: use it as a quick guide, not as a precise calculation.

**Error 3. Confusing 68 % with 95 %.** Some students think that 68 % fall within two standard deviations. No: 68 % fall within one; 95 % fall within two. Detection: if someone swaps the numbers, they are confusing the figures. Correction: memorize the sequence 68-95-99.7 in order: 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations.

**Error 4. Applying the rule to data with heavy tails.** In data with positive kurtosis, more than 5 % of the data will fall outside two standard deviations, not the 5 % that the normal predicts. Detection: if someone applies the rule to financial returns without checking kurtosis, they are underestimating tail risk. Correction: use the rule only with approximately normal data; with heavy tails, use percentiles or the MAD.

**Error 5. Rounding too early.** When computing the intervals, rounding the mean or standard deviation before finishing can shift the interval limits. Detection: compare with the calculation without rounding. Correction: keep decimals in intermediates and round only when presenting.

**Error 6. Believing the empirical rule applies to any "bell".** Not every distribution with a central peak is normal. It can be symmetric but with different tails, or bimodal. Detection: if someone assumes normality just because the histogram has a central peak, they are ignoring the shape of the tails. Correction: verify skewness and kurtosis, not just the peak.

**Error 7. Believing the empirical rule gives exact percentiles.** The rule gives approximations (68, 95, 99.7 %), not exact percentiles for any z. For a z of 1.5, you need a normal table or a calculator, not the empirical rule. Detection: if someone uses the rule to say that z = 1.5 is exactly the 93rd percentile, they are approximating without saying so. Correction: use tables or software for exact percentiles; the empirical rule is only for z = 1, 2, 3.

## 8. Connections and transfer

The empirical rule connects directly with the z-score from the previous lesson: a z of 1, 2 or 3 corresponds respectively to the edges of the 68 %, 95 % and 99.7 % intervals. It also connects with confidence intervals in inference: a 95 % confidence interval is basically "mean ± 2 standard errors", which is a version of the empirical rule applied to the distribution of the sample mean.

It also connects with outlier detection: a z greater than 2 or less than -2 is unusual in a normal distribution, and that is exactly what the empirical rule tells you. And it connects with skewness and kurtosis: if those coefficients deviate much from zero, the empirical rule stops being valid and you should use robust methods.

In professional practice, the empirical rule is used as a quick check: "is this value within two standard deviations of the mean?". If not, it deserves a review. It is a first-line tool for exploratory analysis and quality control.

The most useful transfer is the habit of asking: how many standard deviations separate this value from the mean, and what proportion of the data fall within that range? That question, more than the raw value, tells you whether a datum is typical or unusual.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. A normal distribution has mean 100 and standard deviation 15. What percentage of the data falls between 85 and 115? — **Solution:** Between mean minus one standard deviation and mean plus one standard deviation: approximately 68 %.
2. What percentage falls between 70 and 130 (mean 100, standard deviation 15)? — **Solution:** Between mean minus two standard deviations and mean plus two standard deviations: approximately 95 %.
3. A z-score of 2 corresponds to approximately which percentile in a normal distribution? — **Solution:** Approximately the 97.5th percentile (95 % lie between $z = -2$ and $z = 2$, so 2.5 % lie above $z = 2$).
4. A machine produces parts with mean 10 mm and standard deviation 0.1 mm. Within what range do 99.7 % of the parts fall? — **Solution:** Between $10 - 3\times0.1 = 9.7$ mm and $10 + 3\times0.1 = 10.3$ mm.
5. Can the empirical rule be applied to company salaries (highly skewed)? — **Solution:** No. Salaries have a right tail and are not approximately normal; the empirical rule would give absurd ranges (for example, negative salaries).
6. Explain in two sentences why the empirical rule does not apply to data with heavy tails. — **Solution:** Because in data with heavy tails, more than 5 % of the values fall outside two standard deviations, not the 5 % that the normal predicts. The rule assumes normal tails; with heavy tails, it underestimates the frequency of extremes.
**Map of five ideas.** First: the empirical rule says that, in an approximately normal distribution, 68 % of the data fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 % within two, and 99.7 % within three. Second: it only applies to approximately normal and symmetric distributions; it does not work for skewed data or heavy tails. Third: a z of 1, 2 or 3 corresponds respectively to approximately the 84th, 97.5th and 99.85th percentiles. Fourth: a value with $|z| > 2$ is unusual in a normal distribution; with $|z| > 3$, very unusual. Fifth: the empirical rule is a useful approximation for exploratory analysis and quality control, but it is not an exact law nor does it work for all data.
