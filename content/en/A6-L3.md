## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, compute and interpret Pearson's skewness coefficient $g_1 = 3(\bar{x} - \tilde{x})/s$ and know what its sign means: positive indicates right skew (rare high values), negative indicates left skew (rare low values), close to zero indicates approximate symmetry. Second, compute and interpret the kurtosis (excess) coefficient $g_2$ and distinguish mesocurtic (kurtosis 0, like the normal), leptocurtic (positive kurtosis, heavy tails and sharp peak) and platycurtic (negative kurtosis, light tails and flat peak). Third, explain why kurtosis measures mainly the tails, not the peak, although in practice they often go together. Fourth, connect skewness and kurtosis with the choice of central tendency measure: in skewed distributions, the median describes the typical case better than the mean. Fifth, recognize that strong skewness or high kurtosis put parametric techniques (t-tests, confidence intervals, regression) that assume normality at risk. Sixth, detect the frequent mistake of confusing kurtosis with "high peak" or of thinking that skewness means "erroneous data". Seventh, interpret in real cases (incomes, house prices, financial returns) what the shape of the distribution tells you about the data. Eighth, connect these concepts with the empirical rule (68-95-99.7 %) and with boxplots, which you saw before.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons on mean, median, standard deviation and z-score. You need to know how to compute the mean, the median and the standard deviation, because Pearson's coefficient uses all three. You need to understand what a squared deviation is and what the fourth power is, because kurtosis uses moments of order higher than two. And you should have worked with examples of skewed distributions (incomes, house prices) for the intuition not to be abstract.

Entry check, thirty seconds. A distribution of salaries has mean 58.4 thousand, median 24 thousand and high standard deviation. What is the sign of Pearson's skewness coefficient $g_1 = 3(\bar{x} - \tilde{x})/s$? If you answered positive (right skew), you are ready. A normal distribution has skewness 0 and kurtosis (excess) 0. If you answered that, you are ready. If you answered that kurtosis measures "high peak", you have confused a common mistake; this lesson exists to correct it. If you hesitated, review the mean-median relationship from the previous lesson before continuing.

A note on scope. Skewness and kurtosis are descriptions of shape, not definitive tests of normality. A skewness close to zero does not guarantee that the data are normal (it could be a symmetric bimodal distribution). Use them as diagnosis, not as a sentence.

## 2. Why it matters

The shape of a distribution tells you more than its center and its dispersion by themselves. Two datasets can have the same mean and the same standard deviation, but one be symmetric and the other have a long right tail. That changes completely the interpretation and the decision you take.

Imagine a real estate report saying "the average house price is 450,000 euros and the standard deviation is 100,000". If the distribution has a strong right tail (a few extremely expensive mansions), the mean is pulled upward and does not represent the typical house. Skewness warns you of that. If you ignore the shape and use only mean and standard deviation, you are making decisions about a "typical case" that does not exist.

It also matters in finance. Financial returns often have heavy tails (positive kurtosis): extreme crises occur more frequently than the normal distribution predicts. If you ignore kurtosis and assume normality, you underestimate the risk of collapse. Kurtosis warns you that the tails are heavier than they seem.

It also matters because many statistical techniques (t-tests, confidence intervals, linear regression, ANOVA) assume that the data or the errors are approximately normal, which implies symmetry and kurtosis close to zero. If skewness or kurtosis are strong, those assumptions are at risk and you should use robust or non-parametric methods, or transform the data (for example, with logarithms).

And there is a practical reason: in public policies and business, percentiles and decision thresholds are affected by the shape. If the income distribution is very skewed, the mean income does not tell you what the typical person earns; you need the median and the percentiles.

## 3. Intuition

Picture a line of people ordered by height, from shortest to tallest. In a symmetric distribution, the line looks the same on both sides of the person in the middle: there are as many very short people as very tall people, and the peak is in the center. Mean, median and mode coincide (or nearly so).

Now imagine that a few people are extremely tall (basketball players, for example). The line still has its middle point, but now there is a "tail" stretching to the right: a few very tall people who stretch the distribution. The mean is pulled upward because those high values add a lot; the median barely moves because it is still the person in the middle. That is right skew: mean > median. Salaries and house prices are the classic example: a few very rich people pull the mean upward.

Conversely, imagine that a few people are extremely short. The mean is dragged downward, the median stays put. That is left skew: mean < median. Retirement ages or scores on a very easy exam (almost everyone scores high, few fail) are typical examples.

Now kurtosis. Think of kurtosis as the "weight of the tails", not as the height of the peak. A distribution with heavy tails has more extreme values than the normal predicts: crises, disasters or spectacular successes occur more frequently. A distribution with light tails has fewer extremes than expected. The peak can be high or low, but what kurtosis measures is mainly what the tails weigh.

An extreme case. Stock market returns usually have positive kurtosis (heavy tails): crashes and spikes occur more frequently than the bell curve predicts. If you assume normality, you underestimate the risk of an extreme drop. Kurtosis warns you that the tails are heavier than they seem.

The central intuition is this: skewness tells you which way the tail pulls (and therefore in which direction the mean moves relative to the median); kurtosis tells you whether the tails are heavier or lighter than normal. Together they tell you whether your data behave like a bell curve or whether there is something more complex behind them.

## 4. Definition and notation

**Pearson's skewness (second form).** For a sample with mean $\bar{x}$, median $\tilde{x}$ and standard deviation $s$, Pearson's skewness coefficient is

$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{\bar{x} - \tilde{x}}{s}$$

The sign indicates the direction of skewness:
- $g_1 > 0$: right skew (tail toward high values; mean > median).
- $g_1 < 0$: left skew (tail toward low values; mean < median).
- $g_1 \approx 0$: no skewness detected by this measure (distribution approximately symmetric).

The magnitude indicates the intensity of skewness relative to dispersion. As a practical rule: $|g_1| < 0.5$ indicates light or no skewness; $0.5 \le |g_1| < 1$ indicates moderate skewness; $|g_1| \ge 1$ indicates strong skewness.

**Kurtosis (excess).** Kurtosis measures the weight of the tails of a distribution relative to a normal distribution. The excess kurtosis coefficient is denoted $g_2$ and, for a sample, is computed as

$$g_2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}\right)^4 - 3$$

The $-3$ subtracts the value of the normal distribution (which has kurtosis 3 in its non-excess form, or 0 in its excess form). The interpretation:
- $g_2 \approx 0$: mesocurtic (tails like the normal).
- $g_2 > 0$: leptocurtic (heavier tails than the normal; more extreme values).
- $g_2 < 0$: platycurtic (lighter tails than the normal; fewer extreme values).


| Type | Skewness $g_1$ | Kurtosis $g_2$ | What it indicates | Typical example |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Symmetric | ≈ 0 | ≈ 0 | No dominant tail | Adult heights |
| Right-skewed | > 0 | Variable | Tail toward high values | Salaries, house prices |
| Left-skewed | < 0 | Variable | Tail toward low values | Retirement ages, easy exams |
| Heavy tails | Variable | > 0 | More extremes than normal | Financial returns, crises |
| Light tails | Variable | < 0 | Fewer extremes than normal | Data very concentrated |
**Important precision.** Kurtosis measures mainly the tails, not the peak. Although a distribution with heavy tails often has a higher peak and thinner shoulders, kurtosis does not measure "peak height" directly. That is a common mistake.

## 5. Step-by-step computation of Pearson's skewness

**Step 1. Compute the mean $\bar{x}$ and the median $\tilde{x}$.** You need both.

**Step 2. Compute the standard deviation $s$.** $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}$ (sample).

**Step 3. Compute the numerator $\bar{x} - \tilde{x}$.** If positive, there is right skew; if negative, left skew.

**Step 4. Divide by $s$ and multiply by 3.** That normalizes the difference by dispersion and gives Pearson's coefficient.

**Step 5. Interpret sign and magnitude.** Positive = right tail; negative = left tail; magnitude = intensity.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1. Salaries: right skew.** Five employees earn 20, 22, 24, 26 and 200 thousand euros. The mean is 58.4, the median is 24. The standard deviation is high because of the 200. Suppose $s \approx 74.6$ (computed from the data). Then

$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{58.4 - 24}{74.6} = 3 \cdot \frac{34.4}{74.6} \approx 3 \cdot 0.461 \approx 1.38$$

Positive and above 1: strong right skew. The tail of the 200 salary pulls the mean upward. The median (24) describes the typical employee better than the mean (58.4).

### Example 2. Symmetric distribution.** Data: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. The mean is 6, the median is 6, the standard deviation is $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$. Then

$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{6 - 6}{3.16} = 0$$

No skewness detected. Mean, median and center coincide.

### Example 3. Left skew: retirement ages.** The retirement ages of ten people are: 55, 60, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69. The median is 64.5. The mean is 63.9. The standard deviation is approximately 3.9. Then

$$g_1 = 3 \cdot \frac{63.9 - 64.5}{3.9} = 3 \cdot \frac{-0.6}{3.9} \approx -0.46$$

Negative and close to zero: light left skew. The tail of 55 pulls the mean slightly downward.

### Example 4. Kurtosis: heavy tails vs light tails.** Two distributions with the same mean and standard deviation. One has positive kurtosis (leptocurtic): more extreme values than normal (financial crises, natural disasters). Another has negative kurtosis (platycurtic): fewer extremes than normal (data very concentrated around the mean). Kurtosis tells you what to expect in the tails, even when the mean and standard deviation are equal.

### Example 5. Kurtosis does not measure the peak.** A distribution can have a high peak and light tails (platycurtic), or a low peak and heavy tails (leptocurtic). Do not assume that "high peak = high kurtosis". Kurtosis measures how much the tails weigh, not the height of the peak.

### Example 6. Finance: heavy tails in returns.** Stock market returns usually have positive kurtosis (heavy tails): crashes and spikes occur more frequently than the normal predicts. If you assume normality to calculate risk (value at risk, for example), you underestimate the risk of an extreme drop. Kurtosis warns you that the tails are heavier than they seem.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Assuming skewness means "wrong data" or "something bad".** Skewness is a legitimate distribution shape. Salaries, house prices and waiting times are naturally skewed. Detection: if someone says "the data are wrong because mean and median differ", they are confusing shape with quality. Correction: report skewness as a characteristic, not as a defect, and choose the measure accordingly.

**Error 2. Confusing kurtosis with "high peak".** Kurtosis measures the tails, not the peak. A distribution with a high peak can have light tails, and one with a low peak can have heavy tails. Detection: if someone says "kurtosis high = pointy peak", they are oversimplifying. Correction: remember that kurtosis measures how much the tails weigh.

**Error 3. Believing that skewness zero means normal distribution.** A symmetric distribution can be non-normal (for example, a symmetric bimodal distribution). Detection: if someone says "since skewness is zero, the data are normal", they are overstating. Correction: complement with histograms, kurtosis and normality tests.

**Error 4. Interpreting the sign of $g_1$ backwards.** $g_1 > 0$ is right tail (rare high values), not left tail. Detection: remember that the mean is pulled toward the tail, so if $g_1 > 0$, the mean is above the median and the tail is upward. Correction: visualize the histogram before interpreting the sign.

**Error 5. Using $g_1$ as definitive proof of normality.** $g_1 \approx 0$ does not prove that data are normal; it only says mean and median are close. Detection: if someone says "since $g_1 \approx 0$, data are normal", they are overstating. Correction: complement with histograms and normality tests.

**Error 6. Believing that high kurtosis invalidates all parametric techniques.** High kurtosis suggests caution, but does not automatically invalidate everything. It depends on sample size and technique. Detection: if someone says "since kurtosis is high, nothing parametric can be done", that is excessive. Correction: evaluate case by case; consider transformations (logarithm for right tail) or robust methods.

**Error 7. Confusing Pearson's kurtosis with the moment kurtosis coefficient.** Several kurtosis formulas exist and can give slightly different results. Detection: if you compare results from different programs and they do not match exactly, it may be the kurtosis formula. Correction: declare which formula you use and apply it consistently throughout the analysis.

**Error 8. Interpreting kurtosis without the context of mean and standard deviation.** High kurtosis in data with low dispersion is not the same as in data with high dispersion. Detection: interpret kurtosis together with standard deviation and mean. Correction: report center, dispersion and shape together.

## 8. Connections and transfer

Skewness and kurtosis connect directly with the choice of central tendency measure from the previous lesson. If $g_1$ indicates right skew, the median is the appropriate measure for the typical case; if $g_1 \approx 0$, mean and median are equivalent. That connection makes Pearson's coefficient not an isolated number, but a guide for decision-making.

They also connect with statistical inference. Many parametric techniques (t-tests, confidence intervals, ANOVA, linear regression) assume that the data or the errors are approximately normal, which implies symmetry and kurtosis close to zero. If $g_1$ or $g_2$ indicate strong deviations, those assumptions are at risk and you should use robust or non-parametric methods, or transform the data (for example, logarithm for right tail).

In probability, skewness is a third-order moment of the distribution and kurtosis is a fourth-order moment. Pearson's coefficient is a simplified version of the moment skewness coefficient; Pearson's kurtosis is a simplified version of the moment kurtosis coefficient. Both measure the degree and direction of deviation from symmetry and normality.

In professional practice, the shape of the distribution is used as a quick diagnostic in business, health and public policy reports. Before presenting a mean, the serious analyst compares it with the median and looks at skewness and kurtosis. That transparency is what separates rigorous analysis from selectively using figures to support a preconceived conclusion.

The most useful transfer is the habit of the mental histogram. Before calculating any summary, imagine the shape of the data: is there a long tail toward some side? Are there extreme values in the tails? That image tells you which measure to choose before the calculation can mislead you.
