## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, define what a random experiment, a sample space, and an event are, and distinguish them with concrete examples. Second, compute the probability of an event using the classical definition (equally likely outcomes) and the frequentist definition (relative frequency in the long run). Third, distinguish between mutually exclusive, independent, and dependent events, and know how this affects the computation of joint probabilities. Fourth, apply the addition rule to compute the probability of the union of two events, distinguishing between mutually exclusive and non-mutually exclusive events. Fifth, apply the multiplication rule to compute the probability of the intersection of two events, distinguishing between independent and dependent events. Sixth, compute conditional probabilities using the formal definition and understand their interpretation as probability conditional on given information. Seventh, construct and use contingency tables (two-way tables) to compute joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities. Eighth, recognize the difference between objective probability (frequentist) and subjective probability (Bayesian), and know when each is appropriate.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons on set theory and logic (union, intersection, complement), as well as fractions, percentages, and ratios. You need to understand what a set is and how its elements are represented. You need to know how to operate with fractions and percentages, since probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1 that are often expressed as percentages. And you should have worked with counting examples (multiplicative principle, permutations and combinations) since classical probability is based on counting outcomes.

Entry check, thirty seconds. You roll a fair six-sided die. What is the probability of getting an even number? If you answered 3/6 = 1/2 = 50 %, you are ready. If you answered 3/6 without simplifying, that is also fine, but remember to simplify whenever possible. If you answered 1/6, you have confused "an even number" with "a specific number". Review the concept of sample space before continuing.

A note on scope. Probability is a measure of uncertainty, not a guarantee. A probability of 0.5 does not mean that something will happen exactly half the time in a small sample, but that in the long run, over many repetitions, the relative frequency approaches 0.5. This distinction between theoretical probability and observed frequency is fundamental to avoid falling into the gambler's fallacy.

## 2. Why it matters

Probability is the language of uncertainty. In daily life, we make decisions under uncertainty all the time: should I take an umbrella if there is a 30 % chance of rain? should I insure my house against fire? should I invest in this stock? All these decisions involve evaluating probabilities and risks.

It also matters because probability is the foundation of inferential statistics. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and p-values are built on probability concepts. Without understanding probability, you do not understand why a result is "statistically significant" nor how to interpret a confidence interval.

Moreover, probability is essential in fields such as medicine (diagnostic tests, disease risk), finance (investment risk, value at risk), engineering (system reliability), and data science (probabilistic models, machine learning). Without understanding probability, you cannot correctly interpret results in these fields.

And there is a practical reason: probability helps you make better decisions under uncertainty. By understanding probability, you can evaluate risks more rationally, avoid common fallacies (such as the gambler's fallacy or the gambler's fallacy), and make more informed decisions under uncertainty.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine you have a bag with colored balls: 3 red and 7 blue. If you draw one ball at random, what is the probability that it is red? The answer is 3/10 = 0.3. This means that if you repeat the experiment many times (drawing a ball, noting the color, and putting it back in the bag), approximately 30 % of the times you will draw a red ball.

This image is useful because it allows us to understand probability as a long-run relative frequency. It does not mean that in the next 10 draws you will draw exactly 3 red balls, but that as you repeat the experiment many times, the proportion of times you draw a red ball approaches 0.3.

An extreme case to fix the idea. If you have a box with only red balls (10 red, 0 blue), the probability of drawing a red ball is 1 (or 100 %). If you have a box with only blue balls (0 red, 10 blue), the probability of drawing a red ball is 0 (or 0 %). These are the extreme cases: probability 1 means absolute certainty that it will occur, and probability 0 means absolute certainty that it will not occur.

Another useful image is the sample space as an area. Imagine a square of area 1 that represents all possible outcomes. Each event is a region within the square, and its area represents its probability. The sum of the areas of all possible outcomes is 1 (the complete sample space). This image helps us understand why the probabilities of all possible outcomes add up to 1, and why the probability of an event cannot be greater than 1 or less than 0.

## 4. Definition and notation

**Random experiment.** A process that produces an outcome that cannot be predicted with certainty beforehand, but whose set of possible outcomes is known. Example: rolling a die, tossing a coin, measuring customer arrival time.

**Sample space (Ω).** The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. For example, for rolling a six-sided die, the sample space is Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

**Event.** A subset of the sample space. For example, the event "getting an even number" is E = {2, 4, 6}.

**Probability of an event.** A function P that assigns to each event a number between 0 and 1, such that:
1. 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 for any event A.
2. P(Ω) = 1 (the probability of the sample space is 1).
3. If A and B are mutually exclusive events (cannot occur at the same time), then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Classical probability.** If all outcomes in the sample space are equally likely, the probability of an event A is:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes favorable to A}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}} = \frac{n(A)}{n(\Omega)}$$

**Frequentist probability.** The probability of an event A is the limit of the relative frequency of A as the number of repetitions of the experiment tends to infinity:

$$P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_A}{n}$$

where n_A is the number of times that A occurs in n repetitions of the experiment.

**Addition rule.** For any two events A and B:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

If A and B are mutually exclusive (A ∩ B = ∅), then P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the formula simplifies to P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Multiplication rule.** For any two events A and B:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$$

If A and B are independent, then P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B).

**Conditional probability.** The probability of A given B is defined as:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

provided that P(B) > 0.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1. Identify the sample space.** List all possible outcomes of the experiment. Make sure all outcomes are equally likely if you are going to use the classical definition.

**Step 2. Identify the event of interest.** Determine which outcomes are favorable to the event you are interested in.

**Step 3. Count the favorable outcomes and the total.** Count how many outcomes are favorable to the event and how many outcomes there are in total.

**Step 4. Compute the probability.** Divide the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes.

**Step 5. Interpret the result.** A probability of 0.3 means that the event will occur approximately 30 % of the time in the long run.

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 1. Rolling a die.** A fair die has six faces: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The sample space is Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, and n(Ω) = 6. The event "getting an even number" is A = {2, 4, 6}, and n(A) = 3. Therefore, P(A) = 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5.

**Example 2. Drawing a ball.** A bag has 5 red balls and 5 blue balls. If you draw a ball at random, what is the probability that it is red? The sample space has 10 balls, and 5 are red. Therefore, P(red) = 5/10 = 0.5.

**Example 3. Drawing a card.** A standard deck has 52 cards. What is the probability of drawing an ace? There are 4 aces in a deck of 52 cards, so P(ace) = 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 0.0769.

**Example 4. Dice.** Roll two dice. What is the probability that the sum is 7? The sample space has 6 × 6 = 36 possible outcomes. The outcomes that sum to 7 are: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) — a total of 6 results. Therefore, P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.

**Example 5. Contingency table.** A survey of 40 households about number of cars:

| Cars | Households | Cumulative frequency |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 5 | 5 |
| 1 | 12 | 17 |
| 2 | 15 | 32 |
| 3 | 6 | 38 |
| 4 | 2 | 40 |

$n = 20$. Range: maximum 4, minimum 0, range = 4. Median Q2: positions 10 and 11; both fall in 1 car (cumulative frequency reaches 10 with 1 car and 16 with 2 cars), so Q2 = 1. Q1: position 5 (25 % of 20). Cumulative frequency reaches 5 with 0 cars, so position 5 falls in 1 car: Q1 = 1. Q3: position 30 (75 % of 40). Cumulative frequency reaches 32 with 2 cars, so position 30 falls in 2 cars: Q3 = 2. IQR: $2 - 1 = 1$ car. The IQR says that the central 50 % of households have between 1 and 2 cars; the range (4) includes the household with 4 cars, which is an extreme case.

**Example 5. Tukey's rule with the quartiles from example 4.** Q1 = 1, Q3 = 2, IQR = 1. Lower limit: $1 - 1.5\times1 = -0.5$. Upper limit: $2 + 1.5\times1 = 3.5$. Any household with fewer than -0.5 or more than 3.5 cars is an outlier according to Tukey. Since the maximum is 4 cars, that household with 4 cars is an outlier according to Tukey (even though only one).

**Example 6. Contrasting two groups with the same median.** Group A: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14. Group B: 5, 10, 12, 14, 19. Both have median 12. Range of A: $14 - 10 = 4$. Range of B: $19 - 5 = 14$. IQR of A: Q1 = 11, Q3 = 13, IQR = 2. IQR of B: Q1 = 7.5, Q3 = 16.5, IQR = 9. The median is the same (12), but B is much more dispersed. The range and the IQR capture that difference; the median does not.

**Example 7. The IQR does not break with an extreme outlier.** Back to salaries 20, 22, 24, 26, 200 thousand euros. Range: $200 - 20 = 180$. Q1 = 22, Q3 = 26, IQR = 4. If we change the 200 to 1,000,000, the range becomes 999,980 (absurd), but the IQR remains 4: Q1 and Q3 do not move because there are still two values below and two above them. That is the robustness of the IQR in action.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1. Believing the mean describes the typical case in skewed distributions.** This is the most frequent error. A mean salary of 58.4 thousand in a group where four of five earn less than 30 thousand misleads the reader. Detection: compare mean and median; if they differ a lot, do not use the mean alone for the typical case. Correction: report the median for the typical and the mean only for the total, and say which is which.

**Error 2. Believing that r = 0 means "no relationship".** Correlation measures linear relationship. Two variables can have a non-linear relationship (for example, Y = X²) and a correlation close to zero. Detection: if someone says "the correlation is 0, therefore there is no relationship", they are confusing concepts. Correction: zero correlation only rules out linear relationship.

**Error 3. Believing that a high correlation means one variable causes the other.** A high correlation does not mean that one variable causes the other. They can be related because of a third factor or by coincidence. Detection: if someone says "the correlation is 0.9, therefore X causes Y", they are confusing concepts. Correction: correlation only indicates linear association, not causality.

**Error 4. Using correlation with non-linear data.** Pearson's correlation only measures linear relationship. If data follow a non-linear relationship (for example, quadratic), the correlation can be close to zero even when there is a clear relationship. Detection: if someone uses correlation for data with a clear curve, they are using the wrong tool. Correction: visualize the data with a scatter plot before calculating correlation.

**Error 5. Believing that a high correlation means that one variable predicts the other perfectly.** A correlation of 0.8 is strong, but not perfect. There is unexplained variance. Detection: if someone says "the correlation is 0.9, therefore I can predict Y exactly from X", they are exaggerating. Correction: use $r^2$ to know what proportion of the variance is explained.

**Error 6. Rounding r too early.** A correlation of 0.85 rounded to 0.9 can change the interpretation. Detection: keep decimals in intermediate calculations. Correction: round only when presenting.

**Error 7. Believing that a high correlation means that one variable causes the other.** A high positive correlation does not mean that one variable causes the other. They can be related by a third factor or by coincidence. Detection: if someone says "the correlation is 0.9, therefore X causes Y", they are confusing concepts. Correction: correlation only indicates linear association, not causality.

## 8. Connections and transfer

Correlation connects directly with covariance and standard deviation from previous lessons: correlation is covariance divided by the product of X and Y standard deviations. It is the way to standardize covariance so that it stays between -1 and 1.

It also connects with linear regression that you will see in future lessons: the slope of the regression line of Y on X is $b = r \cdot \frac{s_y}{s_x}$. Correlation and slope have the same sign.

In finance, correlation between asset returns is fundamental for portfolio risk calculation. If two assets have positive correlation, they tend to move together; if they have negative correlation, one tends to go up when the other goes down, reducing total portfolio risk.

In data science, the correlation matrix of a dataset contains correlations between all pairs of features, and is used to understand which variables are related and for feature selection in machine learning models.

The most useful transfer is the habit of asking: when one variable goes up, does the other also go up or down? And how strong is that relationship? That question, more than the numeric value of r, tells you whether two variables have a linear relationship.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. Data: X = (1, 2, 3), Y = (2, 4, 6). What is the Pearson correlation? — **Solution:** r = 1 (perfect positive linear correlation).

2. If r = 0.6, what is r²? — **Solution:** r² = 0.36. 36 % of the variance of Y is explained by X.

3. If r = -0.9, what type of correlation is it? — **Solution:** Strong negative correlation (close to -1).

4. If r = 0.2, what type of correlation is it? — **Solution:** Weak correlation ($|r| < 0.3$).

5. What does r = 0 mean? — **Solution:** No linear relationship between the two variables. There may be a non-linear relationship.

6. What does $r^2 = 0.64$ mean? — **Solution:** 64 % of the variance of Y is explained by X.

**Map of five ideas.** First: Pearson correlation measures the direction and strength of the linear relationship between two variables. Second: r goes from -1 to 1; r = 1 is perfect positive correlation, r = -1 is perfect negative correlation, r = 0 is no linear correlation. Third: $r^2$ measures the proportion of variance of one variable explained by the other. Fourth: correlation does not imply causation and only measures linear relationship. Fifth: correlation is symmetric and dimensionless (it has no units).

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without notes why r = 0.6 implies r² = 0.36, why a correlation of -0.9 is strong and negative, and why a correlation of 0 does not mean "no relationship" but "no linear relationship", then you own this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, review the corresponding example: the formula is short, but the intuition of "do they move in the same direction?" is what stays.

7. A study finds that coffee consumption correlates with heart disease. What is a possible confounder? — **Solution:** Stress. People under stress may drink more coffee and also have higher heart disease risk. The correlation may be due to stress, not coffee itself.

8. Why does correlation between X and Y not tell us which causes which? — **Solution:** Because the correlation is symmetric (r_XY = r_YX). It measures association but not direction of causation. To determine causation you need experiments or additional evidence beyond the correlation itself.
