## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without notes. First, define what a random experiment, a sample space and an event are, and distinguish them with concrete examples. Second, compute the probability of an event using the classical definition (equally likely outcomes) and the frequentist definition (relative frequency in the long run). Third, distinguish between mutually exclusive, independent and dependent events. Fourth, apply the addition rule to compute the probability of the union of two events. Fifth, apply the multiplication rule to compute the probability of the intersection of two events. Sixth, compute conditional probabilities. Seventh, construct and use contingency tables. Eighth, recognize the difference between objective and subjective probability.

The prerequisites are set theory and basic logic, fractions, percentages and ratios, and counting (multiplicative principle, permutations and combinations).

Entry check. You roll a fair six-sided die. What is the probability of getting an even number? If you answered 3/6 = 1/2 = 50 %, you are ready.

A note on scope. Probability measures uncertainty, not guarantees. A probability of 0.5 does not mean something happens exactly half the time in a small sample, but that in the long run, over many repetitions, the relative frequency approaches 0.5.

## 2. Why it matters

Probability is the language of uncertainty. In daily life we make decisions under uncertainty all the time: should I take an umbrella, insure my house, invest in this stock? All these decisions involve evaluating probabilities and risks.

Probability is also the foundation of inferential statistics. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and p-values are built on probability concepts. Without understanding probability you cannot interpret statistical significance or confidence intervals.

Probability is essential in medicine (diagnostic tests, disease risk), finance (investment risk, value at risk), engineering (system reliability) and data science (probabilistic models, machine learning).

## 3. Intuition

Imagine a bag with colored balls: 3 red and 7 blue. If you draw one ball at random, the probability it is red is 3/10 = 0.3. This means that if you repeat the experiment many times (drawing a ball, noting the color, putting it back), approximately 30 % of the time you will draw a red ball.

This image helps understand probability as a long-run relative frequency. It does not mean you will get exactly 3 red balls in the next 10 draws, but that as you repeat the experiment many times, the proportion of red draws approaches 0.3.

An extreme case. If the box has only red balls, the probability is 1 (100 %). If the box has only blue balls, the probability is 0 (0 %). Probability 1 means absolute certainty of occurrence; probability 0 means absolute certainty of non-occurrence.

Another useful image is the sample space as an area. Imagine a square of area 1 representing all possible outcomes. Each event is a region within the square, and its area represents its probability. The sum of all regions is 1 (the complete sample space).

## 4. Definition and notation

**Random experiment.** A process producing an outcome that cannot be predicted with certainty, but whose set of possible outcomes is known.

**Sample space (Ω).** The set of all possible outcomes. For a die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.

**Event.** A subset of the sample space. "Getting an even number" is E = {2, 4, 6}.

**Probability of an event.** A function P assigning to each event a number between 0 and 1 such that:
1. 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.
2. P(Ω) = 1.
3. If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Classical probability:**
$$P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(\Omega)}$$

**Frequentist probability:**
$$P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_A}{n}$$

**Addition rule:**
$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

**Multiplication rule:**
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A)$$

**Conditional probability:**
$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

## 5. Step-by-step computation

**Step 1.** Identify the sample space. **Step 2.** Identify the event. **Step 3.** Count favorable outcomes and total. **Step 4.** Compute the probability. **Step 5.** Interpret the result.

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 1. Rolling a die.** Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Event "even number" = {2, 4, 6}. P(A) = 3/6 = 0.5.

**Example 2. Drawing a ball.** Bag with 5 red and 5 blue balls. P(red) = 5/10 = 0.5.

**Example 3. Drawing a card.** P(ace) = 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 0.077.

**Example 4. Dice.** Two dice, sum = 7. P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.

**Example 5. Contingency table.**

| | Smoker | Non-smoker | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart problem | 30 | 10 | 40 |
| No problem | 20 | 40 | 60 |
| Total | 50 | 50 | 100 |

P(F ∩ C) = 30/100 = 0.3. P(C|F) = 0.3/0.5 = 0.6.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

**Error 1.** Believing P = 0.5 means exact half in small samples. **Error 2.** Gambler's fallacy. **Error 3.** Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A). **Error 4.** Confusing independent with mutually exclusive. **Error 5.** Ignoring base rates.

## 8. Connections and transfer

Probability connects with inferential statistics (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) and with decision-making under uncertainty in finance, insurance, engineering and data science.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. P(roll > 4) = 2/6 = 1/3. — **Solution:** Two favorable outcomes out of six.
2. P(ace or king) = 8/52 = 2/13. — **Solution:** Eight favorable outcomes out of fifty-two.
3. P(sum = 7 with two dice) = 6/36 = 1/6. — **Solution:** Six favorable outcomes out of thirty-six.
4. P(two red without replacement) = (5/10)(4/9) = 2/9. — **Solution:** Multiply the conditional probabilities.
5. Medical test with 1 % prevalence: P(disease | positive) ≈ 8.76 %. — **Solution:** Base rate matters; most positives are false positives with rare conditions.
6. Explain why correlation does not imply causation in two sentences. — **Solution:** Variables can be correlated through a confounder or by coincidence. A high correlation does not mean one causes the other.

**Map of five ideas.** Probability measures uncertainty between 0 and 1; classical probability counts favorable outcomes; addition and multiplication rules compute compound events; conditional probability measures probability given information; correlation does not imply causation.
