## 1. Objectives and prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you should be able to do eight things without looking at notes. First, define a random experiment, a sample space, and an event, and distinguish them with concrete examples. Second, compute the probability of an event using the classical definition (equally likely outcomes) and the frequentist definition (relative frequency in the long run). Third, distinguish between mutually exclusive, independent, and dependent events, and know how each affects the calculation of joint probabilities. Fourth, apply the addition rule to compute the probability of the union of two events, distinguishing mutually exclusive from non-exclusive events. Fifth, apply the multiplication rule to compute the probability of the intersection of two events, distinguishing independent from dependent events. Sixth, compute conditional probabilities using the formal definition and interpret them as probability given certain information. Seventh, construct and use contingency tables (two-way tables) to compute joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities. Eighth, recognize the difference between objective (frequentist) probability and subjective (Bayesian) probability, and know when each is appropriate.

The prerequisites are the previous lessons on sets and basic logic (union, intersection, complement), plus fractions, percentages, and ratios. You need to understand what a set is and how to represent its elements. You need to be able to operate with fractions and percentages, because probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1 often expressed as percentages. And you need to have worked with counting examples (multiplicative principle, permutations, combinations), because classical probability is based on counting outcomes.

Entry check, thirty seconds. You roll a fair six-sided die. What is the probability of getting an even number? If you answered 3/6 = 1/2 = 50 %, you are ready. If you answered 3/6 without simplifying, that is fine too, but always simplify when possible. If you answered 1/6, you have confused "an even number" with "a specific number." Review the concept of sample space before continuing.

A note on scope. Probability measures uncertainty, not a guarantee. A probability of 0.5 does not mean something happens exactly half the time in a small sample, but that in the long run, over many repetitions, the relative frequency approaches 0.5. This distinction between theoretical probability and observed frequency is fundamental to avoid the gambler's fallacy.

## 2. Why it matters

Probability is the language of uncertainty. In daily life we make decisions under uncertainty all the time: should I take an umbrella if there is a 30 % chance of rain? Should I insure my house against fire? Should I invest in this stock? All these decisions involve evaluating probabilities and risks.

It also matters because probability is the foundation of inferential statistics. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and p-values are built on probability concepts. Without understanding probability, you cannot understand why a result is "statistically significant" or how to interpret a confidence interval.

In addition, probability is essential in fields such as medicine (diagnostic tests, disease risk), finance (investment risk, value at risk), engineering (system reliability), and data science (probabilistic models, machine learning). Without understanding probability, you cannot interpret correctly the results of these fields.

And there is a practical reason: probability helps you make better decisions under uncertainty. By understanding probability, you can evaluate risks more rationally, avoid common fallacies (such as the gambler's fallacy), and make more informed decisions in uncertain situations.

There is one more reason specific to this course. Probability is the bridge between descriptive statistics (summarizing data you already have) and inferential statistics (drawing conclusions about a population from a sample). Every tool in the inferential part of this course — confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, regression — assumes a probabilistic model underneath. If the probability layer is shaky, every inferential conclusion built on it is shaky too.

## 3. Intuition

Imagine a box with colored balls: 3 red and 7 blue. If you draw one ball at random, the probability it is red is 3/10 = 0.3. This means that if you repeat the experiment many times (drawing a ball, noting the color, putting it back), approximately 30 % of the time you will draw a red ball.

This image is useful because it lets us understand probability as a long-run relative frequency. It does not mean that in the next 10 draws you will get exactly 3 red balls, but that as you repeat the experiment many times, the proportion of red draws approaches 0.3.

An extreme case to fix the idea. If you have a box with only red balls (10 red, 0 blue), the probability of drawing a red ball is 1 (100 %). If you have a box with only blue balls (0 red, 10 blue), the probability of drawing a red ball is 0 (0 %). These are the extreme cases: probability 1 means absolute certainty that it will occur, and probability 0 means absolute certainty that it will not occur.

Another useful image is the sample space as an area. Imagine a square of area 1 representing all possible outcomes. Each event is a region inside the square, and its area represents its probability. The sum of the areas of all possible outcomes is 1 (the complete sample space). This image helps us understand why the probabilities of all possible outcomes add up to 1, and why the probability of an event cannot be greater than 1 or less than 0.

A subtler image: overlap. Two events can overlap, meaning they can occur at the same time. The probability of the union is not simply the sum of the two probabilities when they overlap, because the overlapping region would be counted twice. That is exactly why the addition rule subtracts the intersection: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). The area intuition makes this subtraction obvious — you add the two regions and remove the part you counted twice.

## 4. Definition and notation

**Random experiment.** A process that produces an outcome that cannot be predicted with certainty in advance, but whose set of possible outcomes is known. Example: rolling a die, tossing a coin, measuring the arrival time of a customer.

**Sample space (Ω).** The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. For a six-sided die, the sample space is Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. For two coins, Ω = {HH, HT, TH, TT}.

**Event.** A subset of the sample space. For example, the event "getting an even number" is E = {2, 4, 6}. The event "getting at least one head with two coins" is {HH, HT, TH}.

**Probability of an event.** A function P assigning to each event a number between 0 and 1, such that:
1. 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 for any event A.
2. P(Ω) = 1 (the probability of the sample space is 1).
3. If A and B are mutually exclusive events (they cannot occur at the same time), then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Classical probability.** If all outcomes in the sample space are equally likely, the probability of an event A is:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes favorable to A}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}} = \frac{n(A)}{n(\Omega)}$$

**Frequentist probability.** The probability of an event A is the limit of the relative frequency of A as the number of repetitions of the experiment tends to infinity:

$$P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_A}{n}$$

where n_A is the number of times A occurs in n repetitions of the experiment.

**Addition rule (addition theorem).** For any two events A and B:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

If A and B are mutually exclusive (A ∩ B = ∅), then P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the formula simplifies to P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Multiplication rule.** For any two events A and B:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$$

If A and B are independent, then P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B).

**Conditional probability.** The probability of A given B is defined as:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

whenever P(B) > 0. Read it as "the probability of A, assuming B has already occurred."

### Formal definition of probability

Probability is defined axiomatically: 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1, P(Ω) = 1, and P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) for mutually exclusive events. From these three axioms everything else follows — the addition rule, the complement rule P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A), and Bayes' theorem. The axioms are not derived; they are the starting assumptions that make probability a coherent mathematical theory.

## 5. Step-by-step computation

### Step 1. Identify the sample space List all possible outcomes of the experiment. Make sure all outcomes are equally likely if you plan to use the classical definition. If they are not equally likely (a loaded die, a biased coin), the classical formula does not apply and you need frequentist or subjective probability instead.

### Step 2. Identify the event of interest Determine which outcomes are favorable to the event you care about. Write the event as a subset of the sample space.

### Step 3. Count favorable outcomes and total Count how many outcomes are favorable to the event and how many outcomes exist in total.

### Step 4. Compute the probability Divide the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes. Simplify the fraction.

### Step 5. Interpret the result A probability of 0.3 means the event will occur approximately 30 % of the time in the long run, not exactly three times in the next ten trials.

### Step 6 (compound events). Decide which rule applies If the question asks for "A or B," use the addition rule (subtracting the intersection if they are not mutually exclusive). If it asks for "A and B," use the multiplication rule (with the conditional probability if they are dependent, or the plain product if independent). If it asks for "A given B," use the conditional probability formula.

**A derivation worth seeing: why the complement rule follows from the axioms.** By axiom 2, P(Ω) = 1. The sample space Ω is the disjoint union of A and its complement Aᶜ (either A occurs or it does not, and both cannot occur simultaneously). By axiom 3, P(Ω) = P(A) + P(Aᶜ). Therefore P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A). This is not an extra assumption; it is a direct consequence of the three axioms, and it is the tool behind "at least one" calculations: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 1. Rolling a die.** A fair die has six faces: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The sample space is Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, and n(Ω) = 6. The event "getting an even number" is A = {2, 4, 6}, and n(A) = 3. Therefore P(A) = 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5.

**Example 2. Drawing a ball.** A bag has 5 red balls and 5 blue balls. If you draw one ball at random, what is the probability it is red? The sample space has 10 balls, and 5 are red. Therefore P(red) = 5/10 = 0.5.

**Example 3. Drawing a card.** A standard deck has 52 cards. What is the probability of drawing an ace? There are 4 aces in a 52-card deck, so P(ace) = 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 0.0769.

**Example 4. Two dice.** You roll two dice. What is the probability the sum is 7? The sample space has 6 × 6 = 36 possible outcomes. The outcomes that sum to 7 are: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) — six outcomes in total. Therefore P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.

**Example 5. Contingency table (realistic data).** A survey of 200 people asked whether they smoke and whether they have heart problems, producing this table:

| | Smoker | Non-smoker | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart problem | 30 | 10 | 40 |
| No heart problem | 70 | 90 | 160 |
| Total | 100 | 100 | 200 |

The probability that a randomly selected person is a smoker and has heart problems is P(S ∩ H) = 30/200 = 0.15. The probability of being a smoker is P(S) = 100/200 = 0.5. The probability of having a heart problem is P(H) = 40/200 = 0.2. The conditional probability of having a heart problem given that the person is a smoker is P(H|S) = P(H ∩ S)/P(S) = 0.15/0.5 = 0.3. Notice that P(H|S) = 0.3 differs from P(H) = 0.2: smoking and heart problems are not independent, because knowing someone is a smoker changes the estimated probability of heart problems.

**Trap example (with replacement versus without).** A bag has 5 red and 5 blue balls. You draw two balls. What is the probability both are red? The answer depends on whether you replace the first ball. With replacement: P(both red) = (5/10) × (5/10) = 0.25. Without replacement: P(first red) = 5/10 = 0.5, and given the first is red, 4 red balls and 9 total remain, so P(second red | first red) = 4/9, and P(both red) = 0.5 × 4/9 = 2/9 ≈ 0.222. The two answers differ because the events are dependent without replacement — the first draw changes the composition of the bag for the second. Always check whether sampling is with or without replacement.

## 7. Errors and misinterpretations

### Error 1. Exact half in a small sample Probability is a long-run proportion, not a short-run guarantee. In ten coin tosses it is perfectly possible to get seven heads and three tails, even though the probability of heads is 0.5. Short samples fluctuate; the law of large numbers only kicks in over many repetitions.

### Error 2. The gambler's fallacy In independent events (such as coin tosses), each trial is independent of the previous ones. If you have gotten heads five times in a row, the probability of heads on the next toss is still 0.5. The coin has no memory.

### Error 3. Confusing P(A\|B) with P(B\|A) P(A|B) is not the same as P(B|A). For example, the probability of having symptoms given you have a disease may be high, but the probability of having the disease given you have symptoms may be low if the disease is rare. This is the heart of Bayes' theorem and the base-rate fallacy.

### Error 4. Independent vs mutually exclusive Two events can be independent (one does not affect the probability of the other) and at the same time not mutually exclusive (they can occur together). For example, rolling two dice: getting a 6 on the first die and getting a 6 on the second die are independent, but they are not mutually exclusive — both can happen at the same time, and P(6 on both) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36.

### Error 5. Ignoring the base rate When interpreting medical tests or diagnoses, it is essential to consider the base rate (prevalence) of the condition. A test with high sensitivity and specificity can still produce many false positives if the disease is rare in the population. A positive test result does not mean you almost certainly have the disease; you must combine sensitivity, specificity, and prevalence via Bayes' theorem.

## 8. Connections and transfer

Probability connects directly with inferential statistics, which you will see in later lessons. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are built on probability concepts: standard error, p-value, and confidence level are all probability concepts.

It also connects with decision-making under uncertainty: in finance, insurance, and risk management, probabilities are used to quantify risk and make informed decisions. Expected value, for instance, multiplies each possible outcome by its probability and sums the results — a tool used everywhere from insurance premiums to project evaluation.

In data science and machine learning, many algorithms (naive Bayes classification, Bayesian networks, language models) are based on probability and conditional probability.

The most useful transfer is the habit of thinking in probabilities rather than absolute certainties. Instead of saying "this will happen," say "there is an X % chance this will happen." That helps you make better decisions and evaluate risk more realistically.

A final bridge: probability links descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics summarizes data you already have (mean, median, histogram). Inferential statistics draws conclusions about a population from a sample, and every inferential tool assumes a probability model underneath. If the probability layer is shaky, every inferential conclusion built on it is shaky too. Mastering this lesson is therefore a prerequisite for the entire inferential part of the course.

## 9. Practice and summary

1. You roll a fair six-sided die. What is the probability of getting a number greater than 4? — **Solution:** The favorable outcomes are {5, 6}, so there are 2 favorable outcomes out of 6 possible. P(X > 4) = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333.

2. You draw one card from a standard 52-card deck. What is the probability it is an ace or a king? — **Solution:** There are 4 aces and 4 kings, so 8 favorable outcomes out of 52. P(ace or king) = 8/52 = 2/13 ≈ 0.154. Since aces and kings are mutually exclusive, no subtraction is needed.

3. You roll two dice. What is the probability the sum is 7? — **Solution:** There are 36 possible outcomes (6 × 6). The outcomes summing to 7 are (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) — 6 favorable outcomes. P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.

4. A bag has 5 red and 5 blue balls. You draw two balls without replacement. What is the probability both are red? — **Solution:** P(first red) = 5/10 = 0.5. Given the first is red, 4 red balls and 9 total remain, so P(second red | first red) = 4/9. The joint probability is P(both red) = (5/10) × (4/9) = 20/90 = 2/9 ≈ 0.222. Note this is lower than drawing with replacement (0.25) because the first draw changes the bag.

5. A medical test has 95 % sensitivity (detects disease when present) and 90 % specificity (detects no disease when absent). Disease prevalence is 1 %. If a person tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease? — **Solution:** Using Bayes' rule: P(disease | positive) = P(positive | disease) × P(disease) / P(positive). P(positive) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.10 × 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.099 = 0.1085. Therefore P(disease | positive) = 0.0095 / 0.1085 ≈ 0.0876, about 8.76 %. Even with a fairly accurate test, if the disease is rare, most positives are false positives. This is the base-rate fallacy in action.

6. Toss a fair coin twice. What is the probability of getting exactly one head? — **Solution:** The sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT}. The favorable cases are HT and TH, so P(exactly one head) = 2/4 = 1/2 = 0.5.

**Map of five ideas.** First: probability measures uncertainty and takes values between 0 and 1. Second: classical probability counts favorable outcomes over total outcomes; frequentist probability estimates probability as long-run relative frequency. Third: the addition rule and the multiplication rule compute probabilities of compound events; subtract the intersection when events overlap. Fourth: conditional probability measures the probability of an event given that another has occurred, and P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A) in general. Fifth: conditional probability does not imply causality in either direction, and correlation does not imply causation.

**Final self-check.** If you can explain without looking why the probability of drawing an ace from a deck is 1/13, why the probability of drawing two reds without replacement is lower than drawing one red, and why a positive medical test does not necessarily mean you have the disease, then you master this lesson. If any of the three sends you back, review the corresponding example: the formula is short, but the intuition of "long-run relative frequency" is what sticks.
